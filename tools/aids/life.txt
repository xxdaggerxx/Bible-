# Daily life, family and law

== Betrothal
kind: life
forms: betroth, betrothed, betrothest, espoused, espouse, pledged to be married, pledged to marry, engaged, promised in marriage, promised to be married, to be married
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 24:50-58; Exod 22:16; Deut 20:7; Deut 22:23-29; Hos 2:19-20; Matt 1:18-20; Luke 1:27; Luke 2:5; 2Cor 11:2
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, Sketches of Jewish Social Life
Betrothal was the binding first stage of marriage, made by agreement between families, often with a bride price, about a year before the wedding. The couple were legally husband and wife, though they did not yet live together; ending it needed a divorce. That is why Joseph, betrothed to Mary, planned to "put her away privately" (Matt 1:19).

== Weddings
kind: life
forms: wedding, weddings, marriage feast, wedding feast, wedding banquet, wedding garment, wedding clothes, wedding hall, wedding guests, bridechamber, bride chamber, children of the bridechamber, friend of the bridegroom
anywhere: yes
refs: Gen 29:22-27; Judg 14:10-20; Ps 45:13-15; Matt 9:15; Matt 22:1-14; Matt 25:1-13; John 2:1-11; John 3:29; Rev 19:7-9
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, Sketches of Jewish Social Life
At the wedding the bridegroom came with his friends, often at night with lamps, to bring the bride to his home, where a feast lasted up to a week. Guests wore festive clothes, and running out of wine was a real shame for the family (John 2). Jesus' parables of the wedding feast and the ten virgins draw on these customs.

A payment or service the bridegroom gave the bride's family at betrothal. Jacob worked seven years for Rachel; Saul asked David for a hundred Philistine foreskins, hoping he would be killed.

== Levirate marriage
kind: life
forms: brother, brother's, husband's brother, brother's wife, raise up seed, raise up offspring, raise up children, perform the duty
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 38:8-10; Deut 25:5-10; Ruth 1:11-13; Matt 22:24-28; Mark 12:19-23; Luke 20:28-33
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
If a man died without a son, his brother was to marry the widow, and their first son would carry on the dead man's name and inherit his land. A brother who refused was shamed at the city gate. The Sadducees used this law to try to trap Jesus with a question about the resurrection.

== The kinsman-redeemer
kind: life
forms: kinsman, kinsmen, near kinsman, next of kin, near relative, close relative, relative, guardian-redeemer, guardian redeemer, family redeemer, redeemer, redeem, redemption, one of our redeemers
anywhere: no
refs: Lev 25:25-34,47-55; Ruth 2:1,20; Ruth 3:9-13; Ruth 4:1-14; Jer 32:6-15; Job 19:25; Isa 59:20
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
The nearest male relative (Hebrew "goel") had the right and duty to help a family member in trouble: to buy back land that had been sold, to free a relative sold into slavery, and to carry on a dead man's name. Boaz did this for Ruth and Naomi. God calls himself Israel's Redeemer, and Christians have long seen Christ in the kinsman who pays the price to set his people free.

== The avenger of blood
kind: life
forms: avenger of blood, revenger of blood, avenger, revenger, city of refuge, cities of refuge
anywhere: no
refs: Num 35:6-34; Deut 4:41-43; Deut 19:1-13; Josh 20; 2Sam 14:7-11
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
In Israel the nearest relative of a person killed was to avenge them. To protect someone who killed by accident, God appointed six cities of refuge; the killer could flee there and be safe until a fair trial, and could go home free when the high priest died.

== Gleaning
kind: life

anywhere: yes
refs: Lev 19:9-10; Lev 23:22; Deut 24:19-22; Ruth 2:2-23; Isa 17:6
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Farmers were not to reap the edges of their fields, gather what fell, or strip their vines and olive trees bare: what was left was for the poor, the widow, the orphan and the foreigner to gather. This is how Ruth, a poor widow from Moab, came to glean in Boaz's field.

== The threshing floor
kind: life
forms: threshing floor, threshingfloor, threshing-floor, threshing floors, floor, threshing, thresh, threshed, threshingfloors
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 50:10; Judg 6:11,37; Ruth 3:2-14; 2Sam 6:6; 2Sam 24:16-25; 1Chr 21:15-28; 2Chr 3:1; Hos 9:1; Mic 4:12; Matt 3:12; Luke 3:17
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
A flat, hard area of earth or rock, often on a hill to catch the wind, where harvested grain was beaten or trampled by oxen to loosen the kernels. Then it was winnowed. Men slept there at harvest to guard the grain, as Boaz did. David bought Araunah's threshing floor, where Solomon later built the temple.

== Winnowing
kind: life
forms: winnow, winnowed, winnoweth, winnowing, winnowing fork, winnowing shovel, fan, fanned
anywhere: no
refs: Ruth 3:2; Isa 30:24; Isa 41:16; Jer 4:11; Jer 15:7; Jer 51:2; Matt 3:12; Luke 3:17
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
After threshing, the grain and straw were tossed into the air with a fork or shovel (the KJV "fan"); the wind blew the light chaff away and the heavy grain fell back. John the Baptist used it as a picture of Christ's judgment, separating the wheat from the chaff.

== The city gate
kind: life
forms: gate, gates, gate of the city, city gate, gate of his city, gates of the city, town gate
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 19:1; Gen 23:10,18; Gen 34:20-24; Deut 21:19; Deut 22:15,24; Deut 25:7; Ruth 4:1,11; 2Sam 15:2; 2Sam 18:24; 2Sam 19:8; 2Kgs 7:1; Job 29:7; Ps 69:12; Ps 127:5; Prov 22:22; Prov 31:23; Amos 5:10-15
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
The gateway of a walled town was its centre of public life: the elders sat there to judge cases and witness agreements, business was done, and news was shared. Boaz settled his right to marry Ruth before ten elders at the gate; the good wife's husband is "known in the gates" (Prov 31:23).

== Elders

anywhere: yes

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Older men who led their families, towns and the nation, judged cases and represented the people. In Jesus' day the elders were part of the council with the chief priests and scribes. In the church, elders (also called overseers) were appointed to lead and care for each congregation.

== Hospitality

anywhere: no
refs: Gen 18:1-8; Gen 19:1-3; Exod 2:20; Judg 19:15-21; 2Kgs 4:8-10; Job 31:32; Luke 7:44-46; Luke 10:38; Rom 12:13; 1Tim 3:2; Heb 13:2; 1Pet 4:9; 3John 1:5-8
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, Sketches of Jewish Social Life
Welcoming travellers and strangers was a sacred duty in the Bible's world, where there were few inns. A host gave water to wash the feet, food, and shelter, and protected the guest. Abraham welcomed three visitors who turned out to be the Lord and angels; Hebrews remembers it: "some have entertained angels unawares".

== Washing feet
kind: life
forms: wash, washed, washing, washeth, feet, wash his feet, wash your feet, washed his feet, washed their feet, wash the feet, washing of feet
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 18:4; Gen 19:2; Gen 24:32; Gen 43:24; Judg 19:21; 1Sam 25:41; Luke 7:38,44; John 13:4-14; 1Tim 5:10
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
People walked dusty roads in sandals, so a host offered water to wash a guest's feet, and the lowest servant did the washing. When Jesus, their Lord and Teacher, washed his disciples' feet, he gave them an example of humble service.

== Reclining at meals
kind: life
forms: reclined, reclining, recline, sat at meat, sat at table, sitting at meat, lay, leaning, lying, leaned, sat down, at table, reclined at table
anywhere: no
refs: Matt 26:7,20; Mark 14:3,18; Luke 7:36-38; Luke 14:7-10; John 12:2; John 13:23-25; John 21:20
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, Sketches of Jewish Social Life
At feasts in Jesus' day guests lay on couches around a low table, leaning on the left elbow with their feet stretched out behind. That is how a woman could come behind Jesus and wash his feet, and how John could lean back against Jesus at the Last Supper. The places nearest the host were the places of honour.

== Covenant-making
kind: life
forms: covenant, cut a covenant, made a covenant, pieces, between the pieces, divided them, cut in two, heap, pillar, witness
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 15:9-18; Gen 21:27-32; Gen 26:28-31; Gen 31:44-54; Exod 24:3-11; 1Sam 18:3-4; Jer 34:18-20
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
A covenant was a solemn binding agreement. It was often sealed by cutting animals in two and walking between the pieces, as if to say, "may this happen to me if I break it"; or by a shared meal, a heap of stones or a pillar as witness. In Genesis 15 God alone passed between the pieces, binding himself to keep his promise to Abraham.

== Oaths
kind: life
forms: swear, sware, swore, sworn, oath, oaths, under my thigh, under his thigh
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 24:2-9; Gen 47:29; Num 30:2; Deut 6:13; Matt 5:33-37; Matt 23:16-22; Heb 6:13-18; Jas 5:12
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
A solemn promise calling on God as witness. Some were made by putting a hand under the other's thigh. Jesus taught his followers to be so truthful that a plain "yes" or "no" is enough, rather than piling up oaths.

== Casting lots
kind: life

Marked stones or sticks drawn or thrown to make a decision, trusting God to decide: "the lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the Lord" (Prov 16:33). The land was divided by lot, priests' duties were chosen by lot, and the apostles chose Matthias so. The soldiers cast lots for Jesus' clothes, as Psalm 22 had said.

== The birthright

refs: Gen 25:29-34; Gen 27:36; Gen 43:33; Deut 21:15-17; 1Chr 5:1-2; Heb 12:16-17
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
The special rights of the firstborn son: a double share of the inheritance and leadership of the family after the father. Esau sold his birthright to Jacob for a meal; Hebrews warns against treating holy things as lightly as he did.

== The father's blessing
kind: life
forms: bless, blessed, blessing, blessings, blesseth
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 27:1-41; Gen 48:8-20; Gen 49:1-28; Deut 33:1; Heb 11:20-21
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Near death, a father solemnly blessed his sons, speaking God's promises over their future. Once given, the blessing could not be taken back, which is why Esau wept bitterly when Jacob received it by a trick.

== Servants and slaves
kind: life
forms: bondman, bondmen, bondwoman, bondmaid, bondmaids, bondservant, bondservants, bond-servant, bondslave, slave, slaves, slavery, manservant, maidservant, menservants, maidservants, handmaid, handmaiden, handmaids, servant girl, slave girl
anywhere: yes
refs: Exod 21:1-11; Lev 25:39-55; Deut 15:12-18; Phlm 1:10-16; 1Cor 7:21-23; Gal 3:28; Eph 6:5-9; Col 4:1
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
In the ancient world many people were servants or slaves, through debt, war or birth. The Law limited it among Israelites: a Hebrew servant went free in the seventh year, and was not to be ruled harshly, for Israel had been slaves in Egypt. The New Testament calls masters to treat slaves justly, and says that in Christ there is "neither bond nor free" (Gal 3:28).

== Concubines
kind: life
forms: concubine, concubines, secondary wife
anywhere: yes
refs: Gen 16:1-3; Gen 22:24; Gen 25:6; Gen 35:22; Judg 19:1-30; 2Sam 3:7; 2Sam 16:21-22; 1Kgs 11:3
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
A secondary wife, often a servant, with fewer rights than a full wife; her children could inherit only if the father allowed. The Bible tells the stories plainly and shows the trouble that came from it, while God's purpose from the beginning was one husband and one wife (Gen 2:24; Matt 19:4-6).

Men who were castrated, often to serve in royal palaces and in charge of the king's women; the word came to mean any high court official. Isaiah promised faithful eunuchs a name in God's house, and the Ethiopian eunuch was one of the first Gentiles to believe.

== Cupbearers

A trusted officer who served the king's wine, tasting it first to guard against poison. Being close to the king, he had great influence, which is how Nehemiah could ask the king of Persia to let him rebuild Jerusalem.

== Mourning and burial
kind: life
forms: mourn, mourned, mourning, mourners, mourneth, wail, wailing, weep, weeping, wept, minstrels, flute, flutes, flute players, pipers, tomb, tombs, sepulchre, sepulchres, sepulcher, grave, spices, linen, cave, burial, bury, buried
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 23:1-20; Gen 50:1-13; 2Sam 3:31-35; Jer 9:17-18; Matt 9:23-24; Matt 27:57-61; Mark 5:38-40; Mark 15:46; Mark 16:1-4; Luke 7:12; Luke 23:53-56; Luke 24:1; John 11:17-44; John 19:38-42; John 20:1-7
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, Sketches of Jewish Social Life
The dead were buried quickly, usually the same day, wrapped in linen with spices, in a family tomb: often a cave or a room cut in rock, closed by a large stone. Families mourned loudly, often hiring flute players and wailing women, and mourning lasted seven days or more. This is the background to the burial of Jesus and the women coming with spices on the third day.

== Shoes and sandals
kind: life

Taking off one's sandals showed reverence on holy ground. Handing over a sandal sealed the transfer of land or rights (Ruth 4:7). Untying a master's sandals was a slave's lowest task; John the Baptist said he was not worthy even to do that for Christ.

== Wells and cisterns
kind: life
forms: well, wells, cistern, cisterns, draw water, drew water, fountain
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 21:25-31; Gen 24:11-20; Gen 26:15-22; Gen 29:2-10; Exod 2:15-17; Jer 2:13; Jer 38:6; John 4:6-15
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
In a dry land wells were precious, dug deep and sometimes fought over. Women drew water at evening, so the well was a meeting place where Abraham's servant found Rebekah, Jacob met Rachel and Moses met Zipporah, and where Jesus met the Samaritan woman at noon. Cisterns were pits cut in rock to store rainwater; an empty one could be used as a prison, as for Joseph and Jeremiah.

== The sheepfold

anywhere: no
refs: Num 32:16; 1Sam 24:3; Ps 78:70; John 10:1-16

A walled enclosure where flocks were kept safe at night, with one opening. Several shepherds might share one fold; each called his own sheep, who knew his voice. Some shepherds slept across the opening themselves, which helps explain Jesus' words "I am the door of the sheep" (John 10:7).

== Flat roofs

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Houses had flat roofs, reached by outside stairs, used for drying crops, sleeping in hot weather, praying and talking. A wall around the edge was required so no one fell. The friends of the paralysed man dug through the roof of earth and branches to lower him to Jesus.

== Millstones
kind: life

anywhere: no
refs: Exod 11:5; Deut 24:6; Judg 9:53; Judg 16:21; Matt 18:6; Matt 24:41; Luke 17:2,35; Rev 18:21-22
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Grain was ground daily between two round stones, usually by women working a hand mill at home. A large millstone turned by a donkey was too heavy to lift, the picture Jesus used of what it would be better to drown with than to cause a little one to stumble. The Law forbade taking a millstone as a pledge, since a family's food depended on it.

Wine was kept in the whole skins of goats, sewn up. New wine still fermenting would stretch a skin; an old, dry skin would burst. Jesus used this to show that the new life he brings cannot be poured into old forms.

A pit cut in rock where grapes were trodden underfoot, with the juice running into a lower vat. Gideon threshed wheat in a winepress to hide from the Midianites. (In the prophets and Revelation it pictures God's judgment: see Symbols.)

A seal was an engraved stone or ring pressed into clay or wax to sign a document or show ownership; a king's seal carried his full authority. Pharaoh gave Joseph his signet ring; the tomb of Jesus was sealed so no one could open it.

Books were long strips of papyrus or leather, rolled on rods, read by unrolling from one side to the other. In the synagogue at Nazareth Jesus was handed the scroll of Isaiah, found the place, read, rolled it up and sat down to teach.

Every Jewish man paid half a shekel a year for the upkeep of the temple, first commanded as a ransom when the people were counted. Jesus paid it for himself and Peter with a coin found in a fish's mouth.

A Hebrew word for an offering given to God. Some declared their money "corban", dedicated to God, so they would not have to use it to support their parents; Jesus condemned this as cancelling God's command to honour father and mother.

The Roman way of putting slaves, rebels and criminals to death: nailed or tied to a wooden cross and left to die slowly in public, often after flogging. It was the most shameful death, and the Law said one hanged on a tree was under God's curse (Deut 21:23; Gal 3:13). Paul preached "Christ crucified" as God's power to save.

An officer in the Roman army in charge of about a hundred soldiers. Several centurions in the New Testament are good men: one whose faith amazed Jesus, the one at the cross who said "truly this was the Son of God", and Cornelius, the first Gentile believer.

The Praetorium was the Roman governor's residence and court. In Jerusalem Pilate judged Jesus there; the Jewish leaders would not go in, so as not to be made unclean before the Passover. In Rome the Praetorian Guard watched Paul in prison, and he wrote that the gospel had become known to them all.

Being a Roman citizen gave valuable rights: a citizen could not be flogged or punished without a trial, and could appeal to Caesar. Paul was born a citizen and used his rights to protect himself and the gospel; he reminded the Philippians that their true citizenship is in heaven.

The Roman emperor counted the people of his provinces in order to tax them. Because of Caesar Augustus' decree, Joseph and Mary travelled to Bethlehem, the town of David's family, where Jesus was born, as Micah had foretold.

Under Roman law an adopted son received all the rights of a natural son, including the father's name and inheritance, and his old debts were cancelled. Paul uses it to describe believers: brought into God's family, able to call him "Abba, Father", and heirs with Christ.

In the temple a low stone wall kept Gentiles out of the inner courts, with notices warning of death for any who passed it. Paul was nearly killed when he was thought to have brought a Gentile in. He writes that Christ has broken down the "middle wall of partition" between Jew and Gentile.

Learned men from the east (perhaps Persia or Babylon) who studied the stars. They followed a star to Bethlehem and worshipped the child Jesus with gold, frankincense and myrrh: the first Gentiles to honour him as king. The Bible does not say how many there were.
