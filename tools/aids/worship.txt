# Worship, the tabernacle and the temple

== The tabernacle

The holy tent Israel made in the wilderness at God's command, where he dwelt among his people. It could be taken down and carried; it stood in a courtyard with the altar and basin, and inside were the Holy Place (with the lampstand, the table of bread and the incense altar) and the Most Holy Place (with the ark). Hebrews calls it a copy and shadow of the true sanctuary in heaven, where Christ ministers.

== The temple
kind: worship
forms: temple, temples, house of the lord, house of god, temple courts, temple of the lord
anywhere: yes
not: John 2:19-21; 1Cor 3:16-17; 1Cor 6:19; 2Cor 6:16; Eph 2:21; Rev 3:12; Rev 7:15; Rev 11:1,19; Rev 14:15,17; Rev 15:5-8; Rev 16:1,17; Rev 21:22; Judg 9:27; 1Sam 5:2,5; 1Sam 31:10; 2Kgs 10:21-27; 2Kgs 11:18; Ezra 1:7; Dan 1:2; Dan 5:2-3; Acts 19:27; Joel 3:5; Hos 8:14

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
God's house in Jerusalem, where the sacrifices were offered. Solomon built the first temple (about 960 BC); Babylon destroyed it in 586 BC. The second temple was built after the exile and greatly enlarged by Herod the Great; this is the temple Jesus knew, with its large courts where people bought animals and changed money. The Romans destroyed it in AD 70, as Jesus had foretold.

== The Most Holy Place
kind: worship
forms: most holy place, holy of holies, holiest of all, the holiest, inner sanctuary, most holy room, the oracle, most holy
anywhere: no
refs: Exod 26:33-34; Lev 16:2; 1Kgs 6:16,19-23; 1Kgs 8:6; 2Chr 3:8-10; 2Chr 5:7; Ezek 41:4; Heb 9:3,7-8,12,25; Heb 10:19
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
The innermost room of the tabernacle and temple, a perfect cube, where the ark of the covenant stood and God's presence dwelt. A thick curtain shut it off; only the high priest could go in, once a year on the Day of Atonement, with blood. Hebrews says Christ's death opened the way into God's presence for all who trust him (Heb 10:19-20).

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
The heavy woven curtain between the Holy Place and the Most Holy Place, embroidered with cherubim. When Jesus died it was torn in two from top to bottom, showing that the way to God was opened. Hebrews calls Christ's body the veil through which we may now come near (Heb 10:20).

A wooden chest overlaid with gold, holding the stone tablets of the Ten Commandments, with Aaron's rod and a jar of manna. Its lid, the mercy seat, had two golden cherubim; God said he would meet with Israel there. It went before Israel through the Jordan and around Jericho, and stood in the Most Holy Place; it was lost when Babylon took Jerusalem.

The gold lid of the ark of the covenant, between the cherubim, where the high priest sprinkled blood on the Day of Atonement to cover the people's sins. Paul uses the same Greek word for Christ, whom God "set forth to be a propitiation" (Rom 3:25): the place where sin is covered and God's mercy met.

== The altar
kind: worship
forms: altar, altars, altar of burnt offering, bronze altar, brasen altar, altar of the lord
anywhere: yes
refs: Gen 8:20; Gen 12:7-8; Gen 22:9; Exod 20:24-26; Exod 27:1-8; Lev 1:5-9; 1Kgs 18:30-38; Matt 5:23-24; Heb 13:10
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
A raised place of earth, stone or bronze where sacrifices were offered to God. Abraham and the patriarchs built altars where God met them; at the tabernacle and temple, the great bronze altar stood in the courtyard and its fire was never to go out. Altars to Baal and other gods were to be torn down.

Projections like horns at the four corners of the altar. Blood of the sin offering was put on them. A person who fled to the altar and took hold of its horns was asking for God's protection from punishment (1 Kgs 1:50).

Sweet-smelling spices burned on the golden altar before the veil, morning and evening, by a priest chosen by lot. Its rising smoke pictured prayer going up to God (Ps 141:2; Rev 8:4). Zechariah was burning incense when the angel told him John the Baptist would be born.

The golden lampstand in the Holy Place, with a central shaft and six branches, its seven lamps kept burning with pure olive oil. It was the only light in the Holy Place. (In Zechariah and Revelation lampstands are symbols: see Symbols.)

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
Twelve loaves, one for each tribe, set out every Sabbath on a gold table in the Holy Place, "before the face" of the Lord. Only priests could eat the old loaves. When David and his men were hungry the priest gave them this bread, and Jesus used it to show that mercy matters more than ritual.

== Sacrifice
kind: worship
forms: sacrifice, sacrifices, sacrificed, sacrificing, sacrificeth
anywhere: yes
refs: Gen 4:3-5; Gen 22:1-14; Lev 1-7; Lev 17:11; 1Sam 15:22; Ps 51:16-17; Hos 6:6; Rom 12:1; Heb 9:22-28; Heb 10:1-14; Heb 13:15-16
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
An offering to God, most often an animal killed and offered on the altar, or grain, wine or oil. The Law set out several kinds: burnt, grain, peace, sin and guilt offerings. The blood of sacrifices made atonement for sin (Lev 17:11), pointing ahead to Christ, who "offered one sacrifice for sins for ever" (Heb 10:12). Christians are to offer themselves, praise and good deeds as living sacrifices.

An animal wholly burned on the altar, nothing kept back: a picture of complete devotion to God. A lamb was offered every morning and every evening for the nation (the "continual" burnt offering).

An offering of fine flour, with oil and frankincense but no yeast or honey, often baked into cakes. A handful was burned on the altar and the rest went to the priests. (The King James "meat offering" means food offering: "meat" then meant food.)

An offering of thanks or a vow, shared: the fat was burned to God, the priests had their portion, and the worshipper and family ate the rest in a joyful meal before the Lord. It pictured peace and fellowship with God.

An offering for sins done without meaning to, or for uncleanness. Its blood was put on the altar's horns or sprinkled before the veil; for the priest or the nation the body was burned outside the camp. Hebrews notes that Jesus too "suffered outside the gate" (Heb 13:12).

An offering for wrongs that could be paid back, such as taking what belonged to God or to a neighbour. The wrongdoer repaid in full, plus a fifth, and offered a ram. Isaiah says the Servant of the Lord would make his life an offering for guilt (Isa 53:10).

Wine poured out at the altar alongside other offerings. Paul twice speaks of his own life being "poured out" like a drink offering, in his ministry and at his coming death (Phil 2:17; 2 Tim 4:6).

Parts of an offering that were lifted up or waved before the Lord, showing they were given to him, and then given back to the priests as their share. The first sheaf of the harvest and the two loaves of Pentecost were waved in the same way.

A red cow without blemish was burned outside the camp, and its ashes mixed with water were sprinkled on anyone made unclean by touching a dead body. Hebrews contrasts this outward cleansing with the blood of Christ, which cleanses the conscience (Heb 9:13-14).

== The scapegoat

anywhere: no
refs: Lev 16:8-10,20-22,26
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
On the Day of Atonement two goats were chosen by lot. One was sacrificed; over the other the high priest confessed all Israel's sins, laying his hands on its head, and it was sent away into the wilderness, carrying their sins far off. It pictures sin removed "as far as the east is from the west" (Ps 103:12).

== The priests
kind: worship
forms: priest, priests, priesthood, priest's, priests'
anywhere: yes
refs: Exod 28:1; Lev 8-10; Num 18:1-7; Deut 33:8-10; Mal 2:7; Luke 1:5-9; Heb 7:11-28; 1Pet 2:5,9; Rev 1:6
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
Aaron and his descendants, of the tribe of Levi, set apart to serve at the altar, offer sacrifices, burn incense, bless the people and teach God's law. In Jesus' day there were so many priests that they served in 24 divisions, a week at a time (Luke 1:8). The New Testament calls Christ the great High Priest and all believers a "royal priesthood" (1 Pet 2:9). Other nations had priests of their own gods too.

== The high priest
kind: worship

anywhere: yes
refs: Exod 28; Lev 16; Lev 21:10-15; Num 35:25; Matt 26:57-66; John 11:49-51; John 18:13-24; Acts 23:2-5; Heb 4:14-5:10; Heb 7:26-28; Heb 9:7,11-12
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
The head of the priests, at first from Aaron's line, who alone entered the Most Holy Place on the Day of Atonement and wore the ephod and breastpiece with the names of the twelve tribes. In Jesus' day the Romans appointed him; Annas and his son-in-law Caiaphas led the trial of Jesus. "Chief priests" were the leading priestly families on the council. Hebrews presents Jesus as the perfect High Priest who offered himself.

== The Levites
kind: worship
forms: levite, levites, levite's, levitical
anywhere: yes
refs: Num 1:47-53; Num 3:5-13; Num 8:5-26; Num 18:21-24; Deut 18:1-8; 1Chr 23:24-32; Neh 8:7-8; Luke 10:32; John 1:19
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, The Temple: Its Ministry and Services
Members of the tribe of Levi, set apart to serve God in place of the firstborn of Israel. They carried the tabernacle, and later served in the temple as gatekeepers, singers, musicians and helpers of the priests; some taught the Law. They received no land of their own but lived on the tithes, in 48 towns spread through the tribes.

== The ephod
kind: worship

anywhere: yes

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
A priestly garment like a vest or apron. The high priest's ephod was of gold, blue, purple and scarlet, with two onyx stones on the shoulders engraved with the names of the tribes, and the breastpiece fastened to it. A simple linen ephod was worn by others serving God, like the boy Samuel and David dancing before the ark. Sometimes it was used to ask God's will, and Gideon's gold ephod became an idol.

== Clean and unclean
kind: worship
forms: unclean, uncleanness, uncleanliness, ceremonially unclean, ritually unclean, clean and unclean
anywhere: yes
not: Matt 15:11,18,20; Mark 7:15-23
refs: Lev 11-15; Num 19:11-22; Isa 6:5; Hag 2:11-14; Mark 7:1-23; Acts 10:9-16,28; Rom 14:14; 2Cor 6:17
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary; Edersheim, Sketches of Jewish Social Life
In the Law, being "unclean" meant being unfit to come to God's sanctuary for a time, through things like touching a dead body, a skin disease, bodily discharges or eating forbidden animals. It was not always sin, but it taught that God is holy and his people must be set apart. Washing, waiting and offerings made a person clean again. Jesus taught that what truly defiles comes from the heart, and declared all foods clean (Mark 7:19).

== Food laws
kind: worship
forms: eat, eaten, food, foods, meat, meats, clean, unclean, common, kosher
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 9:4; Lev 11:1-47; Lev 17:10-14; Deut 14:3-21; Dan 1:8; Mark 7:18-19; Acts 10:9-16; Acts 15:20,29; Rom 14:1-23; 1Cor 8:1-13; Col 2:16; 1Tim 4:3-5
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Israel was to eat only "clean" animals: land animals that chew the cud and have split hooves, fish with fins and scales, and birds not on the forbidden list; no blood was to be eaten. These rules set Israel apart from the nations. In the New Testament God showed Peter that the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile was gone, and Paul taught that no food is unclean in itself.

The Bible's "leprosy" covers several skin diseases (and even mould in cloth or walls). == Circumcision

The cutting away of the foreskin of every Jewish boy on the eighth day, the sign of God's covenant with Abraham and his descendants. "The uncircumcised" meant the Gentiles. The early church decided that Gentile believers did not need to be circumcised; Paul taught that what matters is the "circumcision of the heart" (Rom 2:29).

After giving birth a mother was unclean for 40 days for a son and 80 for a daughter; then she brought a lamb and a pigeon or dove for a burnt offering and a sin offering. Mary offered "a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons", the offering allowed for the poor (Luke 2:24).

The priests washed their hands and feet before serving at the altar. By Jesus' day the Pharisees had added many washings of hands, cups and pots before eating, following "the tradition of the elders". Jesus rebuked them for keeping these while neglecting God's commandments (Mark 7:8).

== The firstborn redeemed
kind: worship
forms: firstborn, firstling, firstlings, first-born, redeem, redeemed, openeth the womb, opens the womb
anywhere: no
refs: Exod 13:1-2,11-16; Exod 34:19-20; Num 3:40-51; Num 18:15-17; Luke 2:22-23
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Because God spared Israel's firstborn in Egypt, every firstborn son and male animal belonged to him. Firstborn animals were sacrificed; firstborn sons were "redeemed", bought back, for five shekels paid to the priests. Mary and Joseph brought the baby Jesus to the temple to present him to the Lord (Luke 2:22-23).

== Tithes
kind: worship
forms: tithe, tithes, tithing, tithed, tenth, tenths, tenth part, a tenth of
anywhere: no
refs: Gen 14:20; Gen 28:22; Lev 27:30-33; Num 18:21-28; Deut 14:22-29; Deut 26:12-15; 2Chr 31:5-12; Neh 10:37-38; Mal 3:8-10; Matt 23:23; Luke 11:42; Luke 18:12; Heb 7:2-9
sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
A tenth of crops and flocks, given to God. Abraham gave a tenth to Melchizedek. The Law gave the tithe to the Levites (who in turn gave a tenth of it to the priests); another tithe was eaten in celebration at the sanctuary, and every third year it was shared with the poor. Jesus said the Pharisees tithed even herbs but neglected justice, mercy and faith.

A solemn promise to God, often to give something or to do something if he answered a prayer, as Hannah vowed her son to the Lord. Vows were voluntary, but once made had to be kept; the Law gave rules for buying back things vowed. Paul once took a vow and cut his hair at its end.

A man or woman who made a special vow to be set apart to God, for a time or for life. A Nazirite drank no wine and ate nothing from the vine, did not cut their hair, and did not go near a dead body. At the end the hair was shaved and burned with an offering. Samson, Samuel and probably John the Baptist were Nazirites from birth.

Putting hands on a person or animal to bless, to heal, to set someone apart for a task, or (for a sacrifice) to show it stood in the worshipper's place. Jacob blessed Joseph's sons so; Moses laid hands on Joshua; the apostles laid hands on those sent out to serve.

Pouring oil on someone or something to set it apart for God. Priests, kings and some prophets were anointed; "Messiah" (Hebrew) and "Christ" (Greek) both mean "the Anointed One". Oil was also put on guests' heads as an honour, used on the sick, and on the bodies of the dead.

God told Israel to write his words "upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates". Jews came to keep this by fixing a small case holding these verses (a mezuzah) to the doorframe, and touching it as they went in and out.

Small leather boxes holding verses of the Law, tied on the forehead and the left arm for prayer, keeping God's command to bind his words "for a sign upon thine hand" and "between thine eyes". Jesus rebuked those who made theirs broad to be seen as holy.

== High places

anywhere: yes

sources: Easton's Bible Dictionary
Places of worship, often on hilltops, with an altar and sometimes a sacred pole or stone. The Canaanites worshipped their gods there; Israel too offered sacrifices at high places, at first to the Lord, but they led to idolatry. Good kings like Hezekiah and Josiah tore them down, so worship would be at the temple alone.
