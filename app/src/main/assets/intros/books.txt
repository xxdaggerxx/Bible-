## 1 Genesis
author: Moses
date: About 1445–1405 BC, during Israel's years in the wilderness
place: Written in the wilderness of Sinai. The events take place in Mesopotamia, Canaan and Egypt.
audience: The people of Israel, newly rescued from Egypt and on their way to the promised land
type: Narrative history, with genealogies and poetry
background: Genesis means "beginning". It covers more time than any other book of the Bible: from the creation of the world to the death of Joseph in Egypt, around 1800 BC. The first eleven chapters describe the early history of humanity: creation, the fall, the flood and the tower of Babel. From chapter 12 the story narrows to one family, beginning with Abraham, whom God called out of Ur of the Chaldeans (in modern Iraq) to go to Canaan. The patriarchs Abraham, Isaac and Jacob lived as nomadic herdsmen among the city-states of Canaan in the Middle Bronze Age. Their way of life, with covenants, birthrights, household gods and marriage customs, fits what is known of that period. The book ends with Jacob's family settled in Egypt under the protection of Joseph, setting the scene for Exodus.
purpose: To tell Israel where they came from and who their God is: the one Creator of all things, who made humanity in his image. It explains how sin entered the world and why God chose Abraham's family, promising that through them all nations would be blessed. For a people about to enter Canaan, it showed that the land was theirs by God's promise to their fathers.
themes: Creation; sin and its spread; God's judgement and mercy; covenant; promise of land, descendants and blessing; faith and obedience; God's providence working through human failures
outline:
- Creation | 1:1-2:25
- The fall and its results | 3:1-5:32
- The flood | 6:1-9:29
- The nations and Babel | 10:1-11:32
- Abraham | 12:1-25:18
- Isaac and Jacob | 25:19-36:43
- Joseph | 37:1-50:26
people: Adam, Eve, Cain, Abel, Seth, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Hagar, Ishmael, Lot, Isaac, Rebekah, Esau, Jacob (Israel), Rachel, Leah, Joseph and his brothers
places: Eden, Ararat, Babel, Ur, Haran, Canaan, Bethel, Hebron, Sodom and Gomorrah, Beersheba, Egypt, Goshen
keyverses: 1:1; 1:27; 3:15; 12:1-3; 15:6; 22:8; 50:20
connections: Jesus is called the "last Adam" (1 Cor 15:45). Paul uses Abraham's faith as the model of being made right with God (Rom 4:3; Gal 3:6). The flood is recalled in 2 Peter 3:5-7, and Melchizedek is explained in Hebrews 7. The promise of Genesis 3:15 is often called the first announcement of the gospel.

## 2 Exodus
author: Moses
date: About 1445–1405 BC
place: Written in the wilderness. The events take place in Egypt, the Sinai desert and at Mount Sinai.
audience: Israel in the wilderness
type: Narrative history and law
background: About 400 years after Joseph, a new king in Egypt enslaved the Israelites, who had grown into a large people in the region of Goshen. On the traditional dating (based on 1 Kings 6:1), the Exodus happened around 1446 BC. God raised up Moses, born a Hebrew but raised in Pharaoh's court, and after forty years as a shepherd in Midian called him at the burning bush. Through ten plagues God showed his power over Egypt and its gods. Then he brought Israel out through the Red Sea to Mount Sinai. There he made a covenant with them, gave the Ten Commandments and laws, and gave instructions for the tabernacle, the portable tent where he would live among them.
purpose: To show how God kept his promise to Abraham by rescuing his descendants from slavery, and how he made them his own people. Exodus gives the foundation of Israel's faith: redemption from slavery, the covenant at Sinai, God's law, and God's presence dwelling with his people.
themes: Redemption; the Passover; God's name and character ("I AM"); covenant; law; worship; God's presence (the tabernacle and his glory); rebellion and forgiveness
outline:
- Slavery in Egypt and Moses' call | 1:1-4:31
- The plagues and the Passover | 5:1-12:36
- The Exodus and the Red Sea | 12:37-15:21
- The journey to Sinai | 15:22-18:27
- The covenant and the law | 19:1-24:18
- Instructions for the tabernacle | 25:1-31:18
- The golden calf and renewal | 32:1-34:35
- Building the tabernacle | 35:1-40:38
people: Moses, Aaron, Miriam, Pharaoh, Jethro, Joshua, Bezalel
places: Egypt, Goshen, Midian, the Red Sea, the wilderness of Sin, Rephidim, Mount Sinai (Horeb)
keyverses: 3:14; 6:6-7; 12:13; 14:13-14; 19:5-6; 20:2-3; 34:6-7; 40:34
connections: Jesus is called "our Passover lamb" (1 Cor 5:7), and the Last Supper was a Passover meal (Luke 22:15). John 1:14 says the Word "dwelt" (literally, pitched his tent) among us. Hebrews 8-10 explains the tabernacle as a picture of Christ's work. The Ten Commandments are repeated in Deuteronomy 5.

## 3 Leviticus
author: Moses
date: About 1445 BC, in the year after the Exodus
place: Given at Mount Sinai
audience: Israel, and especially the priests from the tribe of Levi
type: Law, with a little narrative
background: Leviticus follows directly from the end of Exodus: the tabernacle has just been set up and God's glory has filled it. The whole book takes place at Mount Sinai within about a month. It is named after the Levites, the tribe set apart for worship, and Aaron and his sons as priests. It gives the rules for sacrifices and offerings, the ordination of priests, clean and unclean food, purity, the Day of Atonement, and the calendar of festivals. Many of its laws set Israel apart from the practices of the Canaanites and Egyptians.
purpose: To teach a people now living with a holy God how to approach him and live as his holy people. The sacrifices show that sin brings death and that forgiveness comes through a substitute's blood. The laws of holiness shaped every part of life: worship, food, health, family, work and justice.
themes: Holiness ("Be holy, for I am holy"); sacrifice and atonement; priesthood; clean and unclean; the Day of Atonement; the festivals; loving your neighbour
outline:
- The offerings | 1:1-7:38
- The priests ordained | 8:1-10:20
- Clean and unclean | 11:1-15:33
- The Day of Atonement | 16:1-34
- The holiness code | 17:1-22:33
- The festivals | 23:1-44
- Further laws, the Sabbath year and Jubilee | 24:1-25:55
- Blessings and curses | 26:1-46
- Vows and dedications | 27:1-34
people: Moses, Aaron, Nadab and Abihu, Eleazar, Ithamar
places: Mount Sinai, the tabernacle
keyverses: 11:44; 16:30; 17:11; 19:2; 19:18; 20:26
connections: Jesus called "love your neighbour as yourself" (19:18) the second greatest commandment (Matt 22:39). Hebrews 9-10 explains how the Day of Atonement points to Christ's once-for-all sacrifice. 1 Peter 1:16 quotes "Be holy, for I am holy."

## 4 Numbers
author: Moses
date: About 1405 BC, near the end of the wilderness years
place: Written on the plains of Moab. The events take place in the wilderness between Sinai and Canaan.
audience: The new generation of Israel about to enter Canaan
type: Narrative history, law and census lists
background: Numbers gets its name from the two censuses of Israel's fighting men (chapters 1 and 26). It covers almost 39 years, from Mount Sinai to the plains of Moab across the Jordan from Jericho. Israel set out from Sinai as an organised camp around the tabernacle. But at Kadesh-barnea they refused to enter Canaan after the spies' report, so God sentenced that generation to die in the wilderness. The Hebrew title, "In the Wilderness", sums up the book. It records repeated complaints and rebellions, including Korah's revolt and even Moses' own failure at Meribah, alongside God's patient provision of manna, water and guidance.
purpose: To warn the new generation not to repeat their parents' unbelief, and to show that God remains faithful to his promises even when his people fail. The second census shows that God preserved the nation, ready now to inherit the land.
themes: Faith and unbelief; God's faithfulness and discipline; complaining; leadership; God's guidance (the cloud and fire); holiness of the camp; blessing (Balaam cannot curse Israel)
outline:
- Preparing to leave Sinai | 1:1-10:10
- From Sinai to Kadesh | 10:11-14:45
- The wilderness years | 15:1-19:22
- From Kadesh to Moab | 20:1-25:18
- The new generation prepares | 26:1-36:13
people: Moses, Aaron, Miriam, Joshua, Caleb, Korah, Balaam, Balak, Phinehas, Eleazar
places: Sinai, Paran, Kadesh-barnea, Mount Hor, Edom, Moab, the Jordan
keyverses: 6:24-26; 14:8-9; 14:18; 20:12; 21:8-9; 23:19
connections: Jesus compared his crucifixion to the bronze serpent (John 3:14-15). Paul uses the wilderness rebellions as warnings for Christians (1 Cor 10:1-11), as does Hebrews 3:7-4:11. Balaam appears in 2 Peter 2:15 and Revelation 2:14.

## 5 Deuteronomy
author: Moses (the account of his death in chapter 34 was added, traditionally by Joshua)
date: About 1405 BC, in the last month of Moses' life
place: The plains of Moab, east of the Jordan
audience: The new generation of Israel about to enter Canaan
type: Speeches (sermons), law and poetry
background: Deuteronomy means "second law", because Moses repeats and explains the law for the generation that grew up in the wilderness. The book is a series of speeches given in the last weeks of Moses' life, before Israel crossed the Jordan under Joshua. Its structure is like the treaties of the ancient Near East: history, stipulations, blessings and curses, witnesses and succession. It presents the covenant between God and Israel as a relationship of love and loyalty. The book ends with Moses' song and blessing, and his death on Mount Nebo after seeing the promised land from a distance.
purpose: To call Israel to renew the covenant and to love the Lord with all their heart as they entered the land. Moses reminds them of what God had done, sets out how they must live, and warns that their future in the land depends on their faithfulness.
themes: Loving God with all the heart; remembering; obedience and blessing; one God and one place of worship; care for the poor, the widow and the foreigner; choosing life; God's faithfulness to his promises
outline:
- First speech: looking back | 1:1-4:43
- Second speech: the covenant laws | 4:44-26:19
- Blessings and curses | 27:1-28:68
- Third speech: covenant renewal | 29:1-30:20
- Joshua appointed; the song of Moses | 31:1-32:52
- Moses' blessing and death | 33:1-34:12
people: Moses, Joshua, Caleb, Og, Sihon
places: The plains of Moab, Mount Nebo (Pisgah), Horeb, Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, the Jordan
keyverses: 6:4-5; 7:9; 8:3; 10:12-13; 18:15; 30:19-20; 31:6
connections: Jesus quoted Deuteronomy each time he was tempted (Matt 4:4, 7, 10). He called Deuteronomy 6:5 the greatest commandment (Mark 12:29-30). Peter and Stephen saw Jesus as the prophet like Moses of 18:15 (Acts 3:22; 7:37). Paul uses 30:12-14 in Romans 10:6-8.
## 6 Joshua
author: Joshua, with a few closing notes (such as his death) added by Eleazar and Phinehas, according to Jewish tradition
date: About 1400–1370 BC
place: Canaan
audience: Israel settled in the promised land
type: Narrative history
background: After Moses' death, Joshua son of Nun, his assistant for forty years, led Israel across the Jordan into Canaan around 1406 BC. Canaan was a land of walled city-states, each with its own king, worshipping gods such as Baal and Asherah. The book records the miraculous crossing of the Jordan, the fall of Jericho, and campaigns in the centre, south and north of the land. It then describes how the land was divided among the twelve tribes. It closes with Joshua's farewell speeches and the renewal of the covenant at Shechem.
purpose: To show that God kept his promise to give Abraham's descendants the land, and that Israel's success depended on trusting and obeying him. The defeat at Ai after Achan's sin shows that disobedience brings failure.
themes: God's faithfulness to his promises; courage and obedience; holy war and judgement on Canaanite sin; the inheritance of the land; covenant renewal; choosing whom to serve
outline:
- Entering the land | 1:1-5:15
- Conquering the land | 6:1-12:24
- Dividing the land | 13:1-21:45
- Joshua's farewell and covenant renewal | 22:1-24:33
people: Joshua, Rahab, Achan, Caleb, Eleazar, Phinehas, Adoni-zedek
places: The Jordan, Gilgal, Jericho, Ai, Gibeon, Shiloh, Shechem, Hebron
keyverses: 1:8-9; 21:45; 23:14; 24:15
connections: Rahab is honoured for her faith (Heb 11:31; Jas 2:25) and appears in Jesus' family line (Matt 1:5). Hebrews 4:8 says the rest Joshua gave was not the final rest, which is still to come. Joshua's name is the Hebrew form of "Jesus", meaning "the Lord saves".

## 7 Judges
author: Samuel, according to Jewish tradition
date: Written in the early monarchy, about 1050–1000 BC; the events cover about 1375–1050 BC
place: Canaan
audience: Israel in the early days of the monarchy
type: Narrative history
background: After Joshua's death Israel had no central leader. The tribes failed to drive out the Canaanites and began to worship their gods. The book follows a repeated cycle: Israel sins, God hands them over to enemies (Mesopotamians, Moabites, Canaanites, Midianites, Ammonites, Philistines), they cry out, and God raises up a "judge", a military deliverer, to rescue them. Twelve judges are named, including Deborah, Gideon, Jephthah and Samson. The final chapters show the moral chaos of the time, summed up in the refrain "In those days Israel had no king; everyone did what was right in his own eyes."
purpose: To explain the spiritual decline of Israel after the conquest, to show God's mercy in repeatedly rescuing his people, and to show the need for a faithful king.
themes: The cycle of sin, oppression, repentance and deliverance; God's patience; the danger of compromise with idolatry; God using weak and flawed people; the need for godly leadership
outline:
- Incomplete conquest | 1:1-3:6
- The judges | 3:7-16:31
- Idolatry and moral collapse | 17:1-21:25
people: Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, Barak, Jael, Gideon, Abimelech, Jephthah, Samson, Delilah
places: Canaan, Mount Tabor, the Jezreel valley, Shechem, Gilead, Zorah, Gaza, Shiloh, Gibeah
keyverses: 2:16-19; 6:12; 7:7; 21:25
connections: Hebrews 11:32-34 names Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah among the heroes of faith. The book prepares for 1 Samuel, where Israel asks for a king.

## 8 Ruth
author: Samuel, according to Jewish tradition
date: Written in the time of David (about 1000 BC) or soon after; set in the days of the judges, around 1100 BC
place: Moab and Bethlehem
audience: Israel in the early monarchy
type: Narrative (a short story)
background: During a famine, a man from Bethlehem named Elimelech moved with his wife Naomi and two sons to Moab, a neighbouring nation often hostile to Israel. The men died, leaving Naomi with two Moabite daughters-in-law. Ruth refused to leave Naomi and came with her to Bethlehem, trusting the God of Israel. There she gleaned in the fields of Boaz, a relative of Elimelech. Under Israel's law of the kinsman-redeemer (Leviticus 25; Deuteronomy 25), a close relative could buy back family land and marry a widow to continue the family line. Boaz did this for Ruth, and their son Obed became the grandfather of King David.
purpose: To show God's quiet providence in ordinary lives, his welcome of a foreigner who trusts him, and how he prepared the family line of David.
themes: Loyal love (Hebrew hesed); redemption; providence; the inclusion of the Gentiles; faithfulness in dark times
outline:
- Naomi's loss and Ruth's loyalty | 1:1-22
- Ruth meets Boaz | 2:1-23
- Ruth's request at the threshing floor | 3:1-18
- Boaz redeems; the birth of Obed | 4:1-22
people: Naomi, Ruth, Orpah, Boaz, Obed
places: Moab, Bethlehem
keyverses: 1:16-17; 2:12; 4:14
connections: Ruth is named in the family line of Jesus (Matt 1:5). Boaz the kinsman-redeemer is often seen as a picture of Christ, who redeems his people (Gal 4:4-5).

## 9 1 Samuel
author: Samuel wrote the early part; the prophets Nathan and Gad completed it (1 Chr 29:29)
date: About 930 BC in its final form; events about 1100–1010 BC
place: Israel
audience: Israel under the kings
type: Narrative history
background: 1 and 2 Samuel were one book in the Hebrew Bible. 1 Samuel covers the change from the judges to the monarchy. It begins with the birth of Samuel, the last judge and a great prophet, in answer to Hannah's prayer. The Philistines, a sea people settled on the southern coast with iron weapons, were Israel's main threat and even captured the ark of the covenant. The people demanded a king "like all the nations", and Samuel anointed Saul. Saul began well but disobeyed God, and God rejected him. Samuel then anointed the young shepherd David, who defeated Goliath. The rest of the book follows David's years as a fugitive from the jealous Saul, ending with Saul's death on Mount Gilboa.
purpose: To record the beginning of Israel's monarchy and show that God looks at the heart. It explains why God rejected Saul and chose David.
themes: God's sovereignty over kings; obedience is better than sacrifice; God looks at the heart; prayer; faithfulness under trial; friendship (David and Jonathan)
outline:
- Samuel | 1:1-7:17
- Saul becomes king | 8:1-15:35
- David and Saul | 16:1-31:13
people: Hannah, Eli, Samuel, Saul, Jonathan, David, Goliath, Michal, Abigail, the medium of Endor
places: Shiloh, Ramah, Mizpah, Gibeah, Bethlehem, the valley of Elah, En-gedi, Ziklag, Mount Gilboa
keyverses: 2:2; 3:10; 15:22; 16:7; 17:45-47
connections: Hannah's song (2:1-10) is echoed in Mary's song (Luke 1:46-55). Paul summarises this history in Acts 13:20-22. David's anointing begins the royal line that leads to Jesus, the Son of David (Matt 1:1).

## 10 2 Samuel
author: Nathan and Gad, the prophets (1 Chr 29:29)
date: About 930 BC in its final form; events about 1010–970 BC
place: Israel, mainly Hebron and Jerusalem
audience: Israel under the kings
type: Narrative history, with poetry
background: 2 Samuel records David's forty-year reign. He ruled first over Judah from Hebron for seven years, then over all Israel. He captured Jerusalem from the Jebusites and made it his capital, bringing the ark there. God made a covenant with David, promising that his house and kingdom would last for ever. David defeated Israel's enemies and expanded the kingdom. But his adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah brought God's judgement: violence within his own family, Amnon's crime, and Absalom's rebellion. The book ends with David's song of praise, his last words and a census that brought a plague.
purpose: To show David as the model king, a man after God's own heart who repented when he sinned, and to record God's covenant with him. It also shows the serious consequences of sin, even for the forgiven.
themes: The Davidic covenant; the kingdom established; sin, repentance and consequences; God's faithfulness; justice and mercy
outline:
- David king over Judah | 1:1-4:12
- David king over all Israel | 5:1-10:19
- David's sin | 11:1-12:31
- Troubles in David's house | 13:1-20:26
- Epilogue: songs and events | 21:1-24:25
people: David, Joab, Abner, Ish-bosheth, Mephibosheth, Bathsheba, Uriah, Nathan, Solomon, Amnon, Tamar, Absalom
places: Hebron, Jerusalem (Zion), Rabbah, Mahanaim, the Jordan
keyverses: 7:12-13; 7:16; 12:13; 22:2-3; 23:3-4
connections: The promise of 7:12-16 is fulfilled in Jesus, the Son of David whose kingdom has no end (Luke 1:32-33; Heb 1:5). Psalm 51 is David's prayer after Nathan confronted him. Psalm 18 is almost the same as 2 Samuel 22.

## 11 1 Kings
author: Jeremiah, according to Jewish tradition; drawing on court records
date: About 560–540 BC, during the exile in Babylon
place: Judah or Babylon
audience: The Jewish exiles
type: Narrative history
background: 1 and 2 Kings were one book. 1 Kings begins with Solomon becoming king around 970 BC. He built the temple in Jerusalem, and Israel reached its height of wealth and influence. But his many foreign wives turned his heart to other gods. After his death around 930 BC the kingdom split: ten northern tribes became Israel under Jeroboam, who set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan; Judah and Benjamin in the south stayed with David's line under Rehoboam. The second half focuses on the northern kingdom under Ahab and Jezebel, who promoted Baal worship, and on the prophet Elijah, who challenged them at Mount Carmel.
purpose: To explain to the exiles why Israel and Judah lost their land: kings and people broke the covenant and worshipped idols. Each king is judged by whether he did right in the Lord's eyes.
themes: Wisdom; the temple and God's presence; faithfulness and idolatry; the divided kingdom; the word of the prophets; God's patience and judgement
outline:
- Solomon's reign | 1:1-11:43
- The kingdom divided | 12:1-14:31
- Kings of Judah and Israel | 15:1-16:34
- Elijah and Ahab | 17:1-22:53
people: David, Solomon, Adonijah, the queen of Sheba, Jeroboam, Rehoboam, Ahab, Jezebel, Elijah, Obadiah, Naboth, Micaiah
places: Jerusalem, Gibeon, Shechem, Bethel, Dan, Samaria, Mount Carmel, Zarephath, Horeb
keyverses: 3:9; 8:27; 9:4-5; 11:11; 18:21; 19:11-12
connections: Jesus said "something greater than Solomon is here" (Matt 12:42). He referred to Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (Luke 4:25-26). James 5:17-18 uses Elijah as an example of prayer.

## 12 2 Kings
author: Jeremiah, according to Jewish tradition; drawing on court records
date: About 560–540 BC
place: Judah or Babylon
audience: The Jewish exiles
type: Narrative history
background: 2 Kings continues from 1 Kings, from about 850 BC to the exile. It begins with Elijah taken up to heaven and the ministry of Elisha. The northern kingdom continued in idolatry despite the prophets' warnings, and in 722 BC Assyria captured Samaria and deported its people. Judah survived longer and had good kings such as Hezekiah, whom God rescued from the Assyrian king Sennacherib, and Josiah, who rediscovered the book of the law and reformed worship. But under Manasseh and later kings Judah also turned to idols. Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem, and in 586 BC destroyed the city and temple and took the people into exile. The book ends with a note of hope: King Jehoiachin released from prison in Babylon.
purpose: To show that the exile was God's just judgement for persistent covenant-breaking, while God kept a lamp burning for David's line.
themes: Judgement for idolatry; the power of God's word through the prophets; reform and revival; God's patience; hope for David's line
outline:
- Elisha's ministry | 1:1-8:15
- Kings of Israel and Judah to the fall of Samaria | 8:16-17:41
- Judah alone to the fall of Jerusalem | 18:1-25:30
people: Elijah, Elisha, Naaman, Jehu, Athaliah, Joash, Hezekiah, Isaiah, Sennacherib, Manasseh, Josiah, Nebuchadnezzar, Zedekiah, Jehoiachin
places: Samaria, Jerusalem, Damascus, Nineveh, Babylon, the Jordan, Shunem
keyverses: 2:11; 5:14-15; 17:13-14; 19:19; 22:19; 23:25
connections: Jesus referred to Naaman's healing (Luke 4:27). John the Baptist came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17). The events of Hezekiah's reign are also in Isaiah 36-39.

## 13 1 Chronicles
author: Ezra, according to Jewish tradition
date: About 450–425 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: The Jews who had returned from exile
type: History, with long genealogies
background: 1 and 2 Chronicles were one book, and in the Hebrew Bible it comes last. It retells Israel's history from a priestly point of view for the small community that had returned from Babylon and rebuilt the temple. 1 Chronicles opens with genealogies from Adam to the returned exiles, showing that the community was connected to Israel's past. It then focuses on David: his reign, bringing the ark to Jerusalem, God's covenant with him, and his detailed preparations for the temple and its worship, including the Levites, singers and gatekeepers.
purpose: To encourage the returned exiles that they were still God's people, heirs of the promises to David, and to show the central importance of the temple and true worship.
themes: God's covenant with David; the temple and worship; the continuity of God's people; seeking the Lord; all Israel united
outline:
- Genealogies from Adam to the exile | 1:1-9:44
- David becomes king | 10:1-12:40
- David brings the ark to Jerusalem | 13:1-16:43
- God's covenant with David; his victories | 17:1-20:8
- Preparations for the temple | 21:1-29:30
people: Adam, Abraham, Jacob, Saul, David, Nathan, Asaph, Solomon
places: Jerusalem (Zion), Hebron, the threshing floor of Ornan (Araunah)
keyverses: 16:8-11; 16:34; 17:11-14; 29:11-13
connections: The genealogies of Matthew 1 and Luke 3 draw on these lists. The promise to David (17:11-14) is fulfilled in Christ (Luke 1:32-33).

## 14 2 Chronicles
author: Ezra, according to Jewish tradition
date: About 450–425 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: The Jews who had returned from exile
type: History
background: 2 Chronicles continues from Solomon's reign to the exile, but it follows only the kings of Judah, David's line. It gives a lot of space to Solomon's building and dedication of the temple, and to kings who reformed worship: Asa, Jehoshaphat, Hezekiah and Josiah. It shows how God responded when kings humbled themselves and sought him, and how disaster followed when they abandoned him. The book ends with the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC and then Cyrus king of Persia's decree in 538 BC allowing the Jews to return and rebuild the temple.
purpose: To teach the returned community that faithfulness to God and his temple brings blessing, and to give hope that God restores his people when they repent.
themes: The temple; seeking God; humility and repentance; reform; God's faithfulness to David's line; restoration
outline:
- Solomon and the temple | 1:1-9:31
- The kings of Judah | 10:1-36:16
- Exile and Cyrus' decree | 36:17-36:23
people: Solomon, Rehoboam, Asa, Jehoshaphat, Joash, Uzziah, Hezekiah, Manasseh, Josiah, Cyrus
places: Jerusalem, the temple, Babylon
keyverses: 7:14; 16:9; 20:15; 20:20; 36:22-23
connections: The last verses continue in Ezra 1:1-3. Jesus referred to the murder of Zechariah (24:20-22), the last martyr in the Hebrew Bible's order (Luke 11:51).

## 15 Ezra
author: Ezra
date: About 440 BC; events about 538–457 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: The returned exiles
type: Narrative history, with official documents
background: Ezra continues where Chronicles ends. In 538 BC Cyrus of Persia allowed the Jews to go home. The first group returned under Zerubbabel, of David's line, and Jeshua the high priest. They rebuilt the altar and began the temple, but opposition from neighbouring peoples stopped the work. Encouraged by the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, they finished the temple in 516 BC. About sixty years later, in 458 BC, Ezra, a priest and teacher of the law, led a second group back with the support of King Artaxerxes. He found that many had married foreign women who worshipped other gods, and he led the people in repentance.
purpose: To show God keeping his promise to bring his people back from exile, and to call them to rebuild their life around his temple and his law.
themes: God's faithfulness in the return; rebuilding the temple; opposition; the word of God; repentance and purity; God moving the hearts of kings
outline:
- The first return and rebuilding the temple | 1:1-6:22
- Ezra's return and reforms | 7:1-10:44
people: Cyrus, Zerubbabel, Jeshua, Haggai, Zechariah, Darius, Artaxerxes, Ezra
places: Babylon, Jerusalem, Persia
keyverses: 1:1; 3:11; 7:10; 9:6
connections: Haggai and Zechariah prophesied during the rebuilding in Ezra 5-6. Nehemiah continues the story. Zerubbabel appears in Jesus' family line (Matt 1:12-13).

## 16 Nehemiah
author: Nehemiah (with Ezra, according to Jewish tradition)
date: About 430 BC; events 445–432 BC
place: Susa (the Persian capital) and Jerusalem
audience: The returned exiles
type: Memoir and history
background: Nehemiah was cupbearer to King Artaxerxes I of Persia, a trusted position. In 445 BC he heard that Jerusalem's walls were still in ruins, leaving the city defenceless and disgraced. After prayer, he asked the king to send him, and he was appointed governor of Judah. Despite mockery, threats and plots from Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem, the people rebuilt the wall in 52 days. Ezra then read the law to the people, who confessed their sins and renewed the covenant. Nehemiah later returned for a second term and corrected abuses.

## 17 Esther

date: About 460–400 BC; events 483–473 BC
place: Susa, the Persian capital
audience: Jews living in the Persian empire
type: Narrative history
background: Esther is set in the reign of Xerxes I (called Ahasuerus), who ruled Persia from India to Ethiopia, between the first and second returns to Jerusalem. Many Jews had stayed in Persia. Esther, a Jewish orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, became queen. When Haman, the king's chief official, plotted to destroy all the Jews, Esther risked her life by going to the king unsummoned. The plot was reversed, Haman was hanged on the gallows he built for Mordecai, and the Jews were saved. The festival of Purim celebrates this deliverance. God is never named in the book, but his hidden hand is seen throughout.

themes: Providence; courage; "for such a time as this"; reversal of fortunes; God's faithfulness to his people

places: Susa, Persia
keyverses: 4:14; 4:16; 8:17

## 18 Job
author: Unknown; Jewish tradition credits Moses
date: The events are set in the time of the patriarchs (about 2000 BC); the time of writing is uncertain
place: The land of Uz, probably east of Canaan near Edom or northern Arabia
audience: God's people in every age
type: Wisdom literature; mostly poetry with a prose opening and ending
background: Job was a wealthy and upright man with a large family. His wealth was counted in livestock, and he offered sacrifices himself as head of the family, as in the time of the patriarchs. In the heavenly court Satan claimed that Job served God only for what he got. God allowed Satan to test him: Job lost his children, his possessions and his health. Three friends, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, came to comfort him but argued that he must be suffering for some sin. Job insisted he was innocent and longed to argue his case with God. A younger man, Elihu, then spoke. Finally God answered Job out of the whirlwind, showing him the wonders of creation. Job repented of speaking beyond his understanding, and God restored his fortunes.

themes: Suffering; the justice and wisdom of God; faith under trial; the limits of human understanding; God's power in creation; restoration

- God speaks from the whirlwind | 38:1-42:6
- Epilogue: Job restored | 42:7-17

places: Uz
keyverses: 1:21; 13:15; 19:25-27; 28:28; 38:4; 42:5-6
connections: James 5:11 holds up Job's perseverance and the Lord's compassion. Ezekiel 14:14 names Job with Noah and Daniel as righteous men. Paul quotes 5:13 in 1 Corinthians 3:19.

## 19 Psalms
author: David wrote about half (73 psalms are headed "of David"). Others are by Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses (Psalm 90), Heman, Ethan and unnamed writers.

audience: Israel at worship, and individual believers
type: Poetry: songs and prayers
background: The Psalms were Israel's songbook and prayer book, used in the temple and in private devotion. They are gathered into five books, each ending with a blessing, perhaps echoing the five books of Moses. Many have headings naming the author, the occasion (for example, when David fled from Absalom) or musical directions. Hebrew poetry works by parallelism: the second line repeats, contrasts or develops the first. There are many kinds of psalm: praise, thanksgiving, lament, confession, trust, wisdom, royal psalms about the king, and songs of ascent sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem.
purpose: To give God's people words to worship him, pour out their hearts to him in every circumstance, and meditate on his word and his works.
themes: Praise; God as king and creator; lament and trust; repentance; the law of the Lord; the anointed king (Messiah); God's steadfast love and faithfulness

- Book 2 | 42:1-72:20
- Book 3 | 73:1-89:52
- Book 4 | 90:1-106:48
- Book 5 | 107:1-150:6

places: Jerusalem (Zion), the temple, Babylon (Psalm 137)
keyverses: 1:1-2; 23:1; 46:1; 51:10; 100:4-5; 119:105; 139:23-24; 150:6
connections: The Psalms are quoted in the New Testament more than any other Old Testament book. Jesus prayed Psalm 22:1 and 31:5 on the cross. Psalm 110 is applied to Christ (Heb 1:13; Acts 2:34-35), as is Psalm 2 (Acts 13:33).

## 20 Proverbs

place: Jerusalem
audience: Young people especially, and everyone who wants to live wisely
type: Wisdom literature: short sayings and poems
background: God gave Solomon great wisdom, and he spoke 3,000 proverbs (1 Kings 4:32). Wisdom writing was known across the ancient Near East, but Proverbs roots all wisdom in "the fear of the Lord". The first nine chapters are a father's longer speeches to his son, and poems in which Wisdom is pictured as a woman calling in the streets. The rest is mostly short two-line sayings on everyday life: work, speech, money, friendship, marriage, pride, anger and justice. The book ends with a poem in praise of a woman of noble character.
purpose: To teach wisdom, the skill of living well in God's world, so that the simple may become wise.
themes: The fear of the Lord; wisdom and folly; the power of words; hard work and laziness; wealth and poverty; family; justice; humility

places: Jerusalem
keyverses: 1:7; 3:5-6; 4:23; 9:10; 16:3; 22:6; 31:30
connections: The picture of Wisdom present at creation (8:22-31) is echoed in what the New Testament says about Christ (1 Cor 1:24, 30; Col 2:3). James is often called the "Proverbs of the New Testament". Hebrews 12:5-6 quotes 3:11-12.

## 21 Ecclesiastes
author: Solomon ("the Teacher, son of David, king in Jerusalem")
date: About 935 BC, late in Solomon's life
place: Jerusalem
audience: Israel, and all who search for meaning
type: Wisdom literature, in reflective prose and poetry
background: The writer calls himself Qoheleth, "the Teacher" or "the Preacher", who gathered people to teach them. As king he had every advantage: wisdom, wealth, great building projects, pleasure and power. He examined all of life "under the sun", that is, from a purely earthly point of view, and found it all "meaningless" or "a breath": fleeting and unable to satisfy. Death comes to wise and foolish alike, and injustice often goes unpunished. Yet he repeatedly commends enjoying food, work and marriage as gifts from God, and concludes that the whole duty of man is to fear God and keep his commandments.
purpose: To show that life without God cannot satisfy, and to lead readers to fear God, enjoy his gifts with gratitude, and live in light of his coming judgement.
themes: The emptiness of life "under the sun"; the limits of wisdom; time and seasons; death; enjoying God's gifts; the fear of God; judgement
outline:
- Everything is meaningless | 1:1-2:26
- A time for everything | 3:1-5:20
- Wisdom and its limits | 6:1-8:17
- Enjoy life and remember God | 9:1-12:8
- Conclusion: fear God | 12:9-14
people: The Teacher (Solomon)
places: Jerusalem
keyverses: 1:2; 3:1; 3:11; 5:2; 12:1; 12:13-14
connections: Paul says creation was subjected to "futility" (the same idea, Rom 8:20) in hope of being set free. Jesus' parable of the rich fool (Luke 12:16-21) echoes the Teacher's point.

## 22 Song of Songs
author: Solomon
date: About 965 BC, early in Solomon's reign
place: Israel: Jerusalem and the countryside
audience: Israel
type: Love poetry
background: The title "Song of Songs" means the greatest song. Solomon wrote 1,005 songs (1 Kings 4:32), and this is the one preserved in Scripture. It is a series of poems between a bride (called the Shulammite) and her beloved, with a chorus of the "daughters of Jerusalem". It celebrates love, desire, courtship and marriage in vivid images drawn from nature: gardens, vineyards, gazelles, spices and the seasons. Jewish readers have traditionally read it as a picture of God's love for Israel, and Christians as a picture of Christ's love for the church. It also stands on its own as a celebration of married love as God's good gift.
purpose: To celebrate the beauty and power of love between husband and wife, and to point to the deeper love between God and his people.
themes: Love and desire; faithfulness; the goodness of marriage; the strength of love ("love is strong as death"); waiting for the right time

- The wedding | 3:6-5:1
- Longing and reunion | 5:2-8:4
- The strength of love | 8:5-14
people: Solomon, the Shulammite bride, the daughters of Jerusalem
places: Jerusalem, Lebanon, En-gedi, Sharon, Tirzah
keyverses: 2:4; 2:16; 8:6-7
connections: The Bible often pictures God's people as his bride (Isa 54:5; Hos 2:19-20; Eph 5:25-32; Rev 19:7).
## 23 Isaiah
author: Isaiah son of Amoz
date: About 740–680 BC

type: Prophecy, mostly in poetry
background: Isaiah was called to be a prophet in the year King Uzziah died (740 BC), in a vision of the Lord's holiness in the temple. He prophesied for about sixty years under Jotham, Ahaz, Hezekiah and possibly Manasseh, who according to tradition had him killed. It was the time when Assyria was the great power: it destroyed the northern kingdom in 722 BC and invaded Judah in 701 BC, when God saved Jerusalem in Hezekiah's reign. Isaiah warned Judah against trusting in foreign alliances instead of God. Looking further ahead, he foretold the exile to Babylon, named Cyrus as the one who would let the people return, and gave comfort to the exiles. He wrote more about the coming Messiah than any other prophet: the child called Immanuel, the king from David's line, and the suffering Servant.

connections: Jesus read Isaiah 61:1-2 in the synagogue at Nazareth and said it was fulfilled in him (Luke 4:17-21). Matthew 1:23 quotes 7:14. Philip explained Isaiah 53 as being about Jesus (Acts 8:32-35). Isaiah is quoted more in the New Testament than any other prophet.

## 24 Jeremiah

type: Prophecy, with narrative
background: Jeremiah was a young priest from Anathoth when God called him in 627 BC, in the reign of Josiah. He prophesied for over forty years through the last kings of Judah: Josiah, Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin and Zedekiah. Assyria was collapsing and Babylon rising. Despite Josiah's reforms, the people returned to idolatry, and Jeremiah warned that Babylon would destroy Jerusalem. He was rejected, beaten, imprisoned and thrown into a cistern, and he is called "the weeping prophet". He saw Jerusalem fall in 586 BC. Afterwards a group of Jews took him to Egypt against his will, where according to tradition he died.

## 25 Lamentations
author: Jeremiah
date: Soon after 586 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: The survivors of Jerusalem's fall
type: Poetry: laments (funeral songs)
background: Lamentations is five poems of mourning over the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple by the Babylonians in 586 BC. The city had been under siege for eighteen months, and its people suffered famine, death and exile. The first four poems are acrostics: each verse or group of verses begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, expressing grief in an ordered way "from A to Z". In the middle of the book, the darkest moment turns to hope in God's unfailing mercies.
purpose: To give voice to grief, to confess that the disaster was God's just judgement for sin, and to turn the people to hope in God's faithfulness.
themes: Grief and suffering; sin and judgement; confession; the faithfulness and compassion of God; hope
outline:
- Jerusalem's desolation | 1:1-22
- The Lord's anger | 2:1-22
- Hope in the midst of suffering | 3:1-66
- The siege remembered | 4:1-22
- A prayer for restoration | 5:1-22
people: Jeremiah
places: Jerusalem (Zion)
keyverses: 1:12; 3:22-23; 3:26; 5:21
connections: Jesus wept over Jerusalem as it faced judgement again (Luke 19:41-44). The words of 1:12 are often applied to Christ's sufferings.

## 26 Ezekiel

audience: The Jewish exiles in Babylon
type: Prophecy, with visions and symbolic actions
background: Ezekiel was among the 10,000 people taken to Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC. Five years later, aged about thirty, he saw an overwhelming vision of God's glory on a throne-chariot and was called as a prophet to the exiles. Many exiles expected a quick return and believed Jerusalem could not fall. Ezekiel acted out dramatic signs and saw visions of God's glory leaving the defiled temple. After news came that Jerusalem had fallen in 586 BC, his message turned to hope: God would give his people a new heart and spirit, bring the dry bones back to life, and dwell among them in a new temple.
purpose: To show that Jerusalem's fall was just, to call each person to personal responsibility and repentance, and to promise restoration, a new heart and God's presence.

## 27 Daniel
author: Daniel
date: About 530 BC; events 605–536 BC
place: Babylon
audience: The Jewish exiles, and God's people facing hostile powers
type: Narrative and apocalyptic prophecy
background: Daniel was a young noble taken from Jerusalem to Babylon in 605 BC by Nebuchadnezzar and trained for royal service. He and his friends Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah (Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego) stayed faithful to God in a pagan court. Daniel served under the Babylonian kings and then the Persian kings Darius the Mede and Cyrus, living through the whole exile. Chapters 1-6 are stories of faithfulness: the fiery furnace, the writing on the wall, the lions' den. Chapters 7-12 are visions of future empires, which traditionally are Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome, and of God's everlasting kingdom and the Son of Man. Part of the book (2:4-7:28) is written in Aramaic, the language of the empire.
purpose: To show that God rules over the kingdoms of men and gives them to whom he will, and to encourage his people to stay faithful under pressure, knowing that his kingdom will triumph.
themes: God's sovereignty over history; faithfulness in a hostile culture; prayer; the kingdoms of the world and the kingdom of God; the Son of Man; resurrection

- Faithfulness in Babylon | 1:1-6:28
- Visions of the future | 7:1-12:13

keyverses: 1:8; 2:20-21; 3:17-18; 4:34; 6:10; 7:13-14; 12:2-3

Revelation shares many of Daniel's images. Hebrews 11:33-34 alludes to the lions' den and the furnace.
## 28 Hosea
author: Hosea son of Beeri
date: About 755–715 BC
place: The northern kingdom of Israel
audience: Israel (also called Ephraim), the northern kingdom
type: Prophecy, with biographical narrative
background: Hosea prophesied in the last decades of the northern kingdom, from the prosperous reign of Jeroboam II through the chaos that followed, when kings were murdered one after another, until shortly before Assyria destroyed Samaria in 722 BC. Israel worshipped Baal, the Canaanite fertility god, alongside the Lord, and trusted in alliances with Assyria and Egypt. God told Hosea to marry Gomer, who was unfaithful to him. Her adultery became a living picture of Israel's unfaithfulness to God. When Hosea bought her back, it showed God's persistent love for his wayward people.

## 29 Joel

## 30 Amos
author: Amos, a shepherd and fig-tree farmer from Tekoa
date: About 760–750 BC
place: Bethel and Samaria in the northern kingdom
audience: Israel, the northern kingdom
type: Prophecy
background: Amos was not a professional prophet but a herdsman from Tekoa in Judah, called by God to go north to Israel. He prophesied "two years before the earthquake" in the reigns of Uzziah of Judah and Jeroboam II of Israel. It was a time of peace and prosperity: the rich built winter and summer houses decorated with ivory, while the poor were cheated, sold into debt slavery and denied justice in the courts. Religious festivals were busy, but worship was empty. Amos announced that God would judge Israel, and within about forty years Assyria destroyed the kingdom.
purpose: To declare that God demands justice and righteousness, not empty religion, and that privilege brings responsibility.
themes: Social justice; God's judgement on all nations; empty worship; the day of the Lord; privilege and responsibility; the future restoration of David's fallen tent

## 31 Obadiah
author: Obadiah (the name means "servant of the Lord")
date: Traditionally soon after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC
place: Judah
audience: Judah, and Edom
type: Prophecy
background: Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament. It is a prophecy against Edom, the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's brother. The Edomites lived in the rugged mountains south-east of the Dead Sea, in cities carved into rock such as Sela (possibly Petra), and they thought themselves unreachable. Despite being Israel's "brother", Edom had a long history of hostility. When Jerusalem was sacked, Edom gloated, looted the city and handed over fleeing survivors. Obadiah announced that Edom would be brought down, while Mount Zion would be delivered and the kingdom would be the Lord's. Edom did later disappear as a nation.
purpose: To announce God's judgement on Edom's pride and cruelty, and to assure God's people that he will vindicate them.
themes: Pride goes before a fall; God's justice ("as you have done, it will be done to you"); the day of the Lord; the kingdom belongs to the Lord
outline:
- Edom's pride and fall | 1:1-9
- Edom's violence against its brother | 1:10-14
- The day of the Lord and Zion's deliverance | 1:15-21
people: Obadiah, Esau (Edom), Jacob
places: Edom, Teman, Mount Seir (Esau), Mount Zion
keyverses: 1:3-4; 1:15; 1:21
connections: The conflict goes back to Jacob and Esau (Gen 25-27). Malachi 1:2-4 and Romans 9:13 speak of God's choice of Jacob over Esau. Herod the Great was an Idumean, a descendant of the Edomites.

## 32 Jonah
author: Jonah son of Amittai
date: About 785–760 BC
place: Israel, the Mediterranean Sea, and Nineveh
audience: Israel
type: Narrative (prophetic history)
background: Jonah was a prophet from Gath-hepher in Galilee during the reign of Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25). God sent him to preach against Nineveh, a great city of Assyria, Israel's brutal enemy. Jonah fled in the opposite direction on a ship bound for Tarshish. After a storm, he was thrown overboard and swallowed by a great fish, prayed from its belly, and after three days was spat out on land. He then preached in Nineveh, and the whole city, from the king down, repented. God spared them. Jonah was angry, because he had known that God is gracious and compassionate. The book ends with God's question to Jonah about his concern for the city.
purpose: To show that God's compassion reaches even Israel's enemies, and to challenge the narrow nationalism of God's people.
themes: God's sovereignty over nature and nations; God's mercy to all peoples; repentance; running from God; the prophet's hard heart
outline:
- Jonah runs from God | 1:1-17
- Jonah's prayer from the fish | 2:1-10
- Nineveh repents | 3:1-10
- Jonah's anger and God's compassion | 4:1-11
people: Jonah, the sailors, the king of Nineveh
places: Gath-hepher, Joppa, Tarshish, Nineveh
keyverses: 2:9; 3:10; 4:2; 4:11
connections: Jesus spoke of "the sign of Jonah": as Jonah was three days in the fish, the Son of Man would be three days in the heart of the earth (Matt 12:39-41). He said the people of Nineveh repented at Jonah's preaching, and "something greater than Jonah is here".

## 33 Micah
author: Micah of Moresheth
date: About 735–700 BC
place: Judah
audience: Samaria and Jerusalem, both kingdoms
type: Prophecy
background: Micah came from Moresheth, a country town in the lowlands of Judah, and prophesied during the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. He was a contemporary of Isaiah. He saw Assyria destroy Samaria in 722 BC and threaten Jerusalem. Like Amos he condemned the rich who seized fields and houses, corrupt judges, priests and prophets who worked for money, and idolatry. He was the first prophet to predict that Jerusalem itself would be ploughed like a field. A century later his words were remembered and saved Jeremiah's life (Jer 26:18-19). Micah also promised a ruler from Bethlehem who would shepherd God's people.
purpose: To warn of judgement for injustice and idolatry, and to promise a coming king from Bethlehem and a restored remnant.
themes: Justice; judgement and hope; the remnant; the ruler from Bethlehem; what the Lord requires; God who pardons sin
outline:
- Judgement on Samaria and Judah | 1:1-3:12
- Future hope: the mountain of the Lord and the king from Bethlehem | 4:1-5:15
- The Lord's case against his people, and hope | 6:1-7:20
people: Micah, Hezekiah
places: Moresheth, Samaria, Jerusalem (Zion), Bethlehem Ephrathah, Assyria
keyverses: 4:2-3; 5:2; 6:8; 7:18-19
connections: The chief priests quoted Micah 5:2 to Herod to say where the Messiah would be born (Matt 2:5-6). Jesus quoted 7:6 about family division (Matt 10:35-36). Micah 4:1-3 is almost identical to Isaiah 2:2-4.

## 34 Nahum
author: Nahum of Elkosh
date: About 663–612 BC
place: Judah
audience: Judah
type: Prophecy, in vivid poetry
background: About a century after Jonah, Nineveh had returned to its violence. Assyria had destroyed Israel, invaded Judah and conquered Thebes in Egypt in 663 BC (mentioned in 3:8). The Assyrians were notorious for their cruelty to conquered peoples. Nahum, whose name means "comfort", announced that Nineveh would fall. His poem describes the city's end in striking detail, including the river gates opened and chariots racing in the streets. Nineveh fell to the Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC and was so completely destroyed that its site was lost for centuries.
purpose: To comfort Judah with the assurance that God would judge the cruel, proud empire that oppressed them.
themes: God's justice and jealousy for his people; God is slow to anger but will not leave the guilty unpunished; God is a refuge; the fall of the proud
outline:
- The Lord's power and justice | 1:1-15
- The fall of Nineveh described | 2:1-13
- Woe to the city of blood | 3:1-19
people: Nahum
places: Elkosh, Nineveh, Thebes (No-amon), Judah
keyverses: 1:3; 1:7; 1:15
connections: Nahum 1:15 ("the feet of him who brings good news") echoes Isaiah 52:7, which Paul applies to preaching the gospel (Rom 10:15). The book is a sequel to Jonah.

## 35 Habakkuk
author: Habakkuk
date: About 609–598 BC
place: Judah
audience: Judah
type: Prophecy: a dialogue with God, ending in a psalm
background: Habakkuk lived in Judah shortly before the Babylonian invasions, probably in the reign of Jehoiakim, when violence and injustice filled the land. Unlike most prophets, he does not speak to the people but to God. He asks why God allows wickedness in Judah to go unpunished. God answers that he is raising up the Babylonians (Chaldeans) to judge Judah. That troubles Habakkuk even more: how can God use a nation more wicked than Judah? God replies that the proud will fall, but "the righteous will live by his faith". The book ends with Habakkuk's prayer of trust: even if the crops fail, he will rejoice in the Lord.
purpose: To show how a believer can wrestle honestly with God over evil and suffering, and come to trust and rejoice in him whatever happens.
themes: Questions about evil; God's sovereignty over the nations; living by faith; woes on the proud; joy in God regardless of circumstances
outline:
- Habakkuk's first complaint and God's answer | 1:1-11
- Habakkuk's second complaint and God's answer | 1:12-2:20
- Habakkuk's prayer | 3:1-19
people: Habakkuk
places: Judah, Babylon (the Chaldeans)
keyverses: 1:13; 2:4; 2:14; 2:20; 3:17-18
connections: "The righteous will live by faith" (2:4) is quoted three times in the New Testament (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11; Heb 10:38) and became central to the Reformation.

## 36 Zephaniah
author: Zephaniah, a descendant of King Hezekiah
date: About 640–625 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: Judah
type: Prophecy
background: Zephaniah prophesied in the reign of Josiah, probably before Josiah's great reform of 622 BC. The previous kings, Manasseh and Amon, had filled Judah with idolatry, including Baal worship and the worship of the stars. As a member of the royal family, Zephaniah spoke to the leaders in Jerusalem. His main theme is "the day of the Lord", a great and terrible day of judgement on Judah and the surrounding nations. But beyond judgement he promises a humble remnant who will seek the Lord, and ends with a picture of God rejoicing over his people with singing.
purpose: To warn of the coming day of the Lord, call people to seek the Lord and humility, and promise joy for a purified remnant.
themes: The day of the Lord; judgement on Judah and the nations; seeking the Lord; the humble remnant; God's joy over his people
outline:
- Judgement on Judah | 1:1-2:3
- Judgement on the nations | 2:4-3:8
- Restoration and rejoicing | 3:9-20
people: Zephaniah, Josiah
places: Jerusalem, Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Cush, Assyria, Nineveh
keyverses: 1:14; 2:3; 3:17
connections: The picture of "the day of the Lord" is developed in the New Testament (1 Thess 5:2; 2 Pet 3:10). The medieval hymn "Dies irae" (Day of Wrath) is based on 1:15.

## 37 Haggai
author: Haggai
date: 520 BC (the prophecies are dated to four months that year)
place: Jerusalem
audience: The returned exiles, Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest
type: Prophecy
background: About 50,000 Jews had returned from Babylon in 538 BC and laid the foundation of the temple, but opposition had stopped the work for sixteen years. Meanwhile, the people had built comfortable, panelled houses for themselves, yet suffered poor harvests and drought. In 520 BC, in the second year of King Darius of Persia, Haggai challenged them: "Is it a time for you to live in panelled houses while this house lies in ruins?" The leaders and people responded and began work within weeks. Haggai encouraged those who felt the new temple was nothing compared to Solomon's, promising that its glory would be greater.
purpose: To stir God's people to put God first and rebuild the temple, and to encourage them with promises of God's presence and future glory.
themes: Priorities ("consider your ways"); God's presence ("I am with you"); obedience and blessing; the future glory of God's house; Zerubbabel as God's chosen signet ring
outline:
- Call to rebuild the temple | 1:1-15
- The future glory of the temple | 2:1-9
- Blessings for a defiled people | 2:10-19
- Promise to Zerubbabel | 2:20-23
people: Haggai, Zerubbabel, Joshua the high priest, Darius
places: Jerusalem
keyverses: 1:5-6; 1:13; 2:4-5; 2:9
connections: Ezra 5:1 and 6:14 record Haggai's ministry. Hebrews 12:26-28 quotes 2:6 about God shaking heaven and earth. Zerubbabel is in Jesus' family line (Matt 1:12).

## 38 Zechariah
author: Zechariah son of Berekiah, son of Iddo, a priest
date: About 520–480 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: The returned exiles
type: Prophecy, with visions and apocalyptic
background: Zechariah began to prophesy two months after Haggai, in 520 BC, and encouraged the same people to complete the temple. Chapters 1-6 are eight night visions: horsemen patrolling the earth, a man measuring Jerusalem, Joshua the high priest given clean clothes, a lampstand fed by two olive trees, a flying scroll and others. They assure the people that God will return to Jerusalem, remove sin and empower the leaders "not by might nor by power, but by my Spirit". Chapters 9-14, probably from later in his life, look further ahead to the coming king, the rejected shepherd, and the day when the Lord will be king over all the earth.
purpose: To encourage the people to finish the temple and return to the Lord, and to give them hope in the coming Messiah and the final triumph of God's kingdom.
themes: Return to the Lord; God's jealous love for Jerusalem; the Spirit; cleansing from sin; the Branch; the humble king; the pierced one; the Lord's universal reign
outline:
- A call to return | 1:1-6
- Eight night visions | 1:7-6:15
- Questions about fasting | 7:1-8:23
- The coming king and the rejected shepherd | 9:1-11:17
- The Lord's final victory | 12:1-14:21
people: Zechariah, Joshua the high priest, Zerubbabel, Darius
places: Jerusalem (Zion), the Mount of Olives
keyverses: 1:3; 4:6; 9:9; 12:10; 13:1; 14:9
connections: Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a donkey, fulfilling 9:9 (Matt 21:4-5). The thirty pieces of silver (11:12-13) are linked to Judas (Matt 27:9-10). Jesus quoted 13:7, "Strike the shepherd" (Matt 26:31), and John 19:37 quotes 12:10, "They will look on him whom they have pierced."

## 39 Malachi
author: Malachi (the name means "my messenger")
date: About 430 BC
place: Jerusalem
audience: The Jews in Judah after the exile
type: Prophecy, as a series of disputes
background: Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament, written about a century after the return and around the time of Nehemiah. The temple had been rebuilt, but the excitement had faded. The people were disappointed that the glorious promises had not yet come true, and their worship had become careless. Priests offered blind and lame animals, people withheld tithes, men divorced their wives and married women who worshipped other gods, and many said it was useless to serve God. Malachi presents God's case in a series of questions and answers. It closes with the promise of a messenger, Elijah, who would prepare the way for the Lord. Then prophecy fell silent for about 400 years until John the Baptist.
purpose: To call a spiritually tired people back to wholehearted love and honour for God, and to promise the coming of the Lord and his messenger.
themes: God's love for his people; honouring God in worship; faithfulness in marriage; tithing; God does not change; the day of the Lord; the coming messenger
outline:
- God's love for Israel | 1:1-5
- The priests' careless worship | 1:6-2:9
- Faithlessness in marriage | 2:10-16
- The coming day of judgement | 2:17-3:5
- Robbing God; the book of remembrance | 3:6-18
- The day of the Lord and Elijah | 4:1-6
people: Malachi, the priests, Elijah
places: Jerusalem, Judah, Edom
keyverses: 1:2; 3:1; 3:6; 3:10; 4:2; 4:5-6
connections: The Gospels identify John the Baptist as the messenger of 3:1 (Mark 1:2; Matt 11:10). Jesus said John was the Elijah who was to come (Matt 11:14; 17:10-13). Luke 1:17 echoes 4:6.
## 40 Matthew
author: Matthew (also called Levi), a tax collector who became one of the twelve apostles
date: About AD 50–60
place: Probably Judea or Syrian Antioch
audience: Jewish Christians
type: Gospel
background: Matthew collected taxes for Herod Antipas at Capernaum, a job that made him despised by fellow Jews, until Jesus called him to follow. His Gospel is placed first in the New Testament because it links the Old Testament and the New: it quotes the Old Testament more than any other Gospel and repeatedly says "this was to fulfil what was spoken by the prophet". It opens with Jesus' family line from Abraham through David. Jesus lived in Roman-ruled Palestine, where Herod's family ruled as client kings, and Pharisees, Sadducees and scribes led Jewish religious life. Matthew arranges Jesus' teaching into five great speeches, beginning with the Sermon on the Mount.
purpose: To show that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of David and King of the Jews promised in the Old Testament, and to teach what it means to be his disciple in the kingdom of heaven.
themes: Jesus as the promised Messiah and King; fulfilment of Scripture; the kingdom of heaven; righteousness; discipleship; the church; the Great Commission to all nations
outline:
- The birth and preparation of the King | 1:1-4:25
- The Sermon on the Mount | 5:1-7:29
- Miracles and mission | 8:1-10:42
- Growing opposition; the parables of the kingdom | 11:1-13:58
- Training the disciples; life in the community | 14:1-18:35
- To Jerusalem; teaching on the end | 19:1-25:46
- The death and resurrection of the King | 26:1-28:20
people: Jesus, Mary, Joseph, the wise men, Herod the Great, John the Baptist, Peter, the twelve apostles, the Pharisees and Sadducees, Caiaphas, Pilate, Judas, Mary Magdalene
places: Bethlehem, Egypt, Nazareth, Galilee, Capernaum, the Sea of Galilee, Caesarea Philippi, Jericho, Jerusalem, Gethsemane, Golgotha
keyverses: 1:21; 5:17; 6:33; 11:28-30; 16:16-18; 22:37-39; 28:18-20
connections: Matthew, Mark and Luke are called the "Synoptic Gospels" because they tell many of the same events in similar order. Compare the Sermon on the Mount with Luke's Sermon on the Plain (Luke 6:20-49). The genealogy draws on 1 Chronicles 1-3.

## 41 Mark
author: John Mark, a companion of Peter and Paul, recording Peter's preaching
date: About AD 55–65
place: Rome
audience: Gentile Christians, especially in Rome
type: Gospel
background: John Mark grew up in Jerusalem, where his mother's house was a meeting place for the church (Acts 12:12). He travelled with Paul and his cousin Barnabas, left them partway through the first journey, and was later reconciled to Paul (2 Tim 4:11). Early church writers such as Papias (about AD 120) say Mark wrote down Peter's preaching accurately. Peter calls him "my son" (1 Pet 5:13). Mark is the shortest Gospel and moves quickly, often using the word "immediately". He explains Jewish customs and translates Aramaic words for his Roman readers. It was written when Christians in Rome faced persecution under Nero.
purpose: To present Jesus as the powerful Son of God who came as a suffering servant to give his life as a ransom for many, and to call disciples to follow him even through suffering.
themes: Jesus the Son of God; his authority over sickness, demons, nature and sin; the servant who suffers; the "messianic secret"; discipleship and taking up the cross; faith and fear
outline:
- The beginning: John the Baptist and Jesus' baptism | 1:1-13
- Ministry in Galilee | 1:14-8:26
- The turning point: Peter's confession | 8:27-9:1
- The way to Jerusalem | 9:2-10:52
- The final week in Jerusalem | 11:1-15:47
- The resurrection | 16:1-20
people: Jesus, John the Baptist, Peter, James and John, the twelve, Herod Antipas, the Pharisees, Pilate, Joseph of Arimathea, Mary Magdalene
places: The Jordan, Galilee, Capernaum, the Sea of Galilee, Caesarea Philippi, Jericho, Bethany, Jerusalem, Gethsemane, Golgotha
keyverses: 1:1; 1:15; 8:34-35; 10:45; 15:39
connections: Almost all of Mark is also found in Matthew or Luke. Peter's sermon in Acts 10:36-43 follows the same outline as Mark's Gospel.

## 42 Luke
author: Luke, a doctor and companion of Paul
date: About AD 60–62
place: Possibly Caesarea or Rome
audience: Theophilus, a Gentile of high standing, and Gentile readers generally
type: Gospel
background: Luke is the only Gentile writer of the New Testament, and his two books, Luke and Acts, make up over a quarter of it. Paul calls him "the beloved physician" (Col 4:14). He travelled with Paul, which is why parts of Acts say "we". Luke tells us he carefully investigated everything from the beginning, relying on eyewitnesses, to write an orderly account. He probably gathered material during Paul's two years in prison at Caesarea. He writes in polished Greek and dates events by Roman rulers. He includes much not found in the other Gospels: the births of John and Jesus, the shepherds, Jesus as a boy in the temple, and parables such as the good Samaritan and the prodigal son.
purpose: To give a reliable, orderly account so that readers may have certainty about Jesus, the Saviour of all people, who came to seek and to save the lost.
themes: Jesus the Saviour of the world; salvation for outsiders (the poor, women, Samaritans, Gentiles, sinners); the Holy Spirit; prayer; joy and praise; the journey to Jerusalem; God's plan fulfilled
outline:
- Prologue | 1:1-4
- The births of John and Jesus | 1:5-2:52
- Preparation for ministry | 3:1-4:13
- Ministry in Galilee | 4:14-9:50
- The journey to Jerusalem | 9:51-19:27
- The final week, death and resurrection | 19:28-24:53
people: Theophilus, Zechariah, Elizabeth, John the Baptist, Mary, Joseph, Simeon, Anna, Jesus, the twelve, Mary and Martha, Zacchaeus, Herod Antipas, Pilate, Cleopas
places: Nazareth, Bethlehem, Jerusalem, the temple, Galilee, Capernaum, Samaria, Jericho, Bethany, Emmaus
keyverses: 1:1-4; 2:10-11; 4:18-19; 15:7; 19:10; 24:46-47
connections: Acts is volume two of Luke's work (Acts 1:1). Luke's family line of Jesus goes back to Adam (3:23-38), showing he came for all humanity. Paul quotes Luke 10:7 as Scripture (1 Tim 5:18).

## 43 John
author: John the apostle, son of Zebedee, "the disciple whom Jesus loved"
date: About AD 85–95
place: Ephesus
audience: Christians in the churches of Asia Minor, and seekers
type: Gospel
background: John and his brother James were fishermen on the Sea of Galilee until Jesus called them. With Peter, they were part of Jesus' inner circle. John was at the cross, where Jesus entrusted his mother to him, and was one of the first to the empty tomb. According to early church writers such as Irenaeus, he lived to old age in Ephesus and wrote his Gospel there, after the other three. About 90% of John's material is not found in the other Gospels. He focuses on Jesus' ministry in Judea and Jerusalem at the Jewish festivals, on long conversations and speeches, and on seven chosen "signs" (miracles) that reveal who Jesus is.
purpose: John states it himself: "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name" (20:31).
themes: Jesus the eternal Word made flesh; the seven signs; the seven "I am" sayings; believing; eternal life; light and darkness; love; the Holy Spirit (the Helper); glory
outline:
- Prologue: the Word | 1:1-18
- The book of signs | 1:19-12:50
- The farewell discourse | 13:1-17:26
- The death and resurrection | 18:1-20:31
- Epilogue: Peter restored | 21:1-25
people: Jesus, John the Baptist, Andrew, Peter, Philip, Nathanael, Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, Lazarus, Martha and Mary, Thomas, Judas, Caiaphas, Pilate, Mary Magdalene
places: Bethany beyond the Jordan, Cana, Jerusalem, the temple, Samaria (Sychar), the Sea of Galilee (Tiberias), Bethany, Golgotha
keyverses: 1:1; 1:14; 3:16; 11:25-26; 14:6; 17:3; 20:31
connections: John's opening echoes Genesis 1:1. The "I am" sayings echo God's name in Exodus 3:14. John also wrote 1-3 John and Revelation. Compare Jesus' prayer in John 17 with the Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9-13).

## 44 Acts
author: Luke
date: About AD 62
place: Probably Rome
audience: Theophilus, and the wider church
type: Narrative history
background: Acts is the second volume of Luke's work. It begins with Jesus' ascension about AD 30 and ends about thirty years later with Paul under house arrest in Rome, still preaching. It ends there probably because that was when Luke wrote. It tells how the church grew from a small group in Jerusalem to congregations across the Roman empire. The Roman peace, good roads, and the Greek language spoken widely helped the gospel spread, as did Jewish synagogues in many cities. The first half centres on Peter and the church in Jerusalem, the second on Paul's missionary journeys among the Gentiles.
purpose: To show how the risen Jesus, by the Holy Spirit, spread the gospel "in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (1:8), and to show that the faith was not a threat to Rome.
themes: The Holy Spirit; witness; the resurrection; the church; the gospel to the Gentiles; prayer; suffering and boldness; God's sovereignty over opposition
outline:
- Witness in Jerusalem | 1:1-7:60
- Witness in Judea and Samaria | 8:1-12:25
- Paul's first journey and the Jerusalem council | 13:1-15:35
- Paul's second and third journeys | 15:36-21:16
- Paul arrested and taken to Rome | 21:17-28:31
people: Peter, John, Stephen, Philip, Saul (Paul), Barnabas, Cornelius, James, Silas, Timothy, Lydia, Priscilla and Aquila, Apollos, Felix, Festus, Agrippa
places: Jerusalem, Samaria, Damascus, Antioch, Cyprus, Galatia, Philippi, Thessalonica, Athens, Corinth, Ephesus, Caesarea, Malta, Rome
keyverses: 1:8; 2:38; 4:12; 9:15; 15:11; 16:31; 20:24
connections: Paul's letters fit into the story of Acts: for example 1 and 2 Thessalonians were written from Corinth (Acts 18), and Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians and Philemon during his imprisonment (Acts 28). Peter's Pentecost sermon quotes Joel 2 and Psalm 16.
## 45 Romans
author: Paul, with Tertius writing it down (16:22)
date: About AD 57
place: Corinth, near the end of Paul's third missionary journey
audience: The church in Rome, made up of Jewish and Gentile believers
type: Letter
background: Paul had never visited Rome, but he hoped to go there on his way to Spain, after taking a gift from the Gentile churches to the poor believers in Jerusalem. The church in Rome had probably begun when Jews from Rome heard the gospel at Pentecost. Around AD 49 the emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome, and when they returned after his death in AD 54, the church, now mostly Gentile, faced tensions between Jewish and Gentile believers. Paul wrote the fullest explanation of the gospel in the New Testament to introduce himself, to set out the message he preached, and to unite the church.
purpose: To explain the gospel: all have sinned, and God makes people right with himself by grace through faith in Jesus Christ. It also shows how Jews and Gentiles fit into God's plan and how the gospel shapes daily life.
themes: The righteousness of God; sin and judgement; justification by faith; union with Christ; life in the Spirit; God's plan for Israel and the nations; living sacrifices; love and unity
outline:
- The gospel introduced | 1:1-17
- All have sinned | 1:18-3:20
- Made right by faith | 3:21-5:21
- New life in Christ and the Spirit | 6:1-8:39
- God's plan for Israel | 9:1-11:36
- Living the gospel | 12:1-15:13
- Paul's plans and greetings | 15:14-16:27
people: Paul, Phoebe, Priscilla and Aquila, Tertius, Abraham, Adam
places: Rome, Corinth (Cenchreae), Jerusalem, Spain
keyverses: 1:16-17; 3:23-24; 5:1; 5:8; 6:23; 8:1; 8:28; 10:9; 12:1-2
connections: Galatians covers similar ground more briefly. Paul's planned visit to Rome happened, though as a prisoner (Acts 28). Romans 4 draws on Genesis 15:6, and Romans 1:17 quotes Habakkuk 2:4.

## 46 1 Corinthians
author: Paul, with Sosthenes
date: About AD 55
place: Ephesus, during Paul's third missionary journey
audience: The church in Corinth
type: Letter
background: Corinth was a wealthy port city in Greece, rebuilt by Rome in 44 BC, and capital of the province of Achaia. It was famous for trade, the Isthmian games, many temples and loose morals. Paul had started the church there on his second journey and stayed eighteen months (Acts 18). Later, while in Ephesus, he heard reports of problems and received a letter with questions from the church. The church was gifted but divided into factions following different leaders, tolerated sexual immorality, took each other to court, and was confused about marriage, food offered to idols, worship, spiritual gifts and the resurrection.
purpose: To correct divisions and disorder, to answer the church's questions, and to call them to live as God's holy people, united in love and centred on Christ crucified and risen.
themes: Unity in Christ; the wisdom of the cross; holiness; freedom and love; the Lord's Supper; spiritual gifts; love; the resurrection
outline:
- Divisions and the message of the cross | 1:1-4:21
- Moral problems | 5:1-6:20
- Marriage and singleness | 7:1-40
- Food offered to idols and Christian freedom | 8:1-11:1
- Order in worship and the Lord's Supper | 11:2-34
- Spiritual gifts and love | 12:1-14:40
- The resurrection | 15:1-58
- The collection and final greetings | 16:1-24
people: Paul, Sosthenes, Apollos, Cephas (Peter), Chloe, Stephanas, Timothy
places: Corinth, Ephesus, Macedonia, Jerusalem
keyverses: 1:18; 2:2; 6:19-20; 10:13; 13:4-7; 13:13; 15:3-4; 15:57-58
connections: Acts 18 tells how the church began. 2 Corinthians continues the story. Paul's account of the Lord's Supper (11:23-26) is the earliest written, and 15:3-8 is one of the earliest summaries of the gospel.

## 47 2 Corinthians
author: Paul, with Timothy
date: About AD 56
place: Macedonia (perhaps Philippi)
audience: The church in Corinth and believers in Achaia
type: Letter
background: After 1 Corinthians, relations between Paul and the Corinthians grew worse. Paul made a painful visit and then wrote a severe letter, sent with Titus. While waiting anxiously in Macedonia, he met Titus and heard that most of the church had repented. Relieved, he wrote this letter. But some in Corinth had been influenced by visiting teachers, whom Paul sarcastically calls "super-apostles", who boasted of their credentials and attacked his authority and appearance. This is Paul's most personal letter, revealing his sufferings, his weaknesses and his love for the church.
purpose: To express Paul's joy at their repentance, to defend his ministry as a true apostle, to encourage generous giving for the poor in Jerusalem, and to warn against false apostles.
themes: Comfort in suffering; the new covenant ministry; strength in weakness; reconciliation; generous giving; true and false apostles
outline:
- Paul's ministry explained | 1:1-7:16
- The collection for Jerusalem | 8:1-9:15
- Paul defends his apostleship | 10:1-13:14
people: Paul, Timothy, Titus
places: Corinth, Macedonia, Troas, Jerusalem, Damascus
keyverses: 1:3-4; 4:7; 4:16-18; 5:17; 5:21; 9:7; 12:9
connections: The collection is mentioned in Romans 15:25-27 and 1 Corinthians 16:1-4. Paul's escape from Damascus in a basket (11:32-33) is also in Acts 9:23-25.

## 48 Galatians
author: Paul
date: About AD 49, just before the Jerusalem council (on the "South Galatia" view)
place: Syrian Antioch
audience: The churches in the province of Galatia, founded on Paul's first journey (Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra, Derbe)
type: Letter
background: Paul and Barnabas had planted churches in southern Galatia, in modern Turkey (Acts 13-14). Afterwards, teachers from Judea came and taught Gentile believers that to be truly saved they must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses. Paul saw this as a different gospel, one that denied salvation by grace. He wrote urgently and forcefully, the only letter in which he gives no thanks for the readers. The question was settled at the Jerusalem council in Acts 15.
purpose: To defend the gospel of grace: people are made right with God by faith in Christ, not by keeping the law, and they are free to live by the Spirit.
themes: Justification by faith; the one true gospel; Paul's authority; freedom in Christ; the promise to Abraham; law and grace; life by the Spirit; the fruit of the Spirit
outline:
- Paul's gospel defended | 1:1-2:21
- Faith, not law | 3:1-4:31
- Freedom in the Spirit | 5:1-6:18
people: Paul, Barnabas, Peter (Cephas), James, John, Titus, Abraham, Hagar and Sarah

## 49 Ephesians

type: Letter
background: Ephesus was the leading city of the Roman province of Asia, home of the great temple of Artemis (one of the wonders of the ancient world) and a centre of magic. Paul spent nearly three years there on his third journey, and the gospel spread through the whole province (Acts 19). He wrote Ephesians while under house arrest in Rome, and sent it with Tychicus, along with Colossians and Philemon. Unusually, it addresses no specific problems, and some early manuscripts omit "in Ephesus", suggesting it was meant to be passed around the churches.

## 50 Philippians

It was the first city in Europe where Paul preached, on his second journey about AD 50 (Acts 16). There Lydia believed, and Paul and Silas were beaten and imprisoned, then freed by an earthquake, and the jailer believed. The Philippians loved Paul and supported him financially more than once. When they heard of his imprisonment, they sent Epaphroditus with a gift. Epaphroditus fell seriously ill, and on his recovery Paul sent him back with this warm letter.

## 51 Colossians
author: Paul, with Timothy
date: About AD 60–62
place: Rome, in prison
audience: The church in Colossae
type: Letter
background: Colossae was a small town in the Lycus valley in Asia Minor, near Laodicea and Hierapolis. Paul had not visited it. The church was founded by Epaphras, probably converted during Paul's ministry in Ephesus. Epaphras came to Paul in Rome with news of a dangerous teaching in the church. It seems to have mixed Jewish rules (food laws, festivals, Sabbaths) with the worship of angels, special visions and harsh self-discipline, claiming to offer a fuller spiritual experience than Christ alone. Paul sent the letter with Tychicus and Onesimus, along with the letter to Philemon.
purpose: To show that Christ is supreme over all things and completely sufficient, so believers need nothing added to him, and to show how new life in him changes everyday life.

## 53 2 Thessalonians

Some had become confused, perhaps by a letter falsely claiming to be from Paul, thinking that "the day of the Lord" had already come. Others had stopped working, perhaps thinking the end was so near that work did not matter, and were living off others. ## 55 2 Timothy

background: This is Paul's last letter. He was again a prisoner in Rome, this time in harsher conditions, probably during Nero's persecution after the great fire of Rome in AD 64. Most of his friends had deserted him or gone elsewhere, and only Luke was with him. He expected to be executed soon; tradition says he was beheaded in Rome. He wrote to Timothy, still in Ephesus, asking him to come quickly and bring his cloak and scrolls, and giving him a final charge to guard the gospel and keep preaching.

## 56 Titus

background: Titus was a Greek convert and trusted co-worker of Paul, who took him to Jerusalem as an example of a Gentile believer who did not need circumcision (Gal 2:1-3). He also handled the difficult situation in Corinth. After his first imprisonment, Paul visited Crete with Titus and left him there to organise the churches. Cretans had a reputation, even among their own poets, as "liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (1:12). False teachers, especially "of the circumcision", were upsetting whole households.

## 57 Philemon
author: Paul, with Timothy
date: About AD 60–62
place: Rome, in prison
audience: Philemon, a believer in Colossae, and the church meeting in his house
type: Letter
background: Philemon was a wealthy Christian in Colossae, converted through Paul, and the church met in his home. His slave Onesimus had run away, possibly stealing from him, and made his way to Rome. There he met Paul and became a Christian. Under Roman law, a runaway slave could be severely punished. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon with this short, personal letter, carried along with Colossians. He asked Philemon to welcome Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, as a beloved brother", and offered to pay anything owed.
purpose: To ask Philemon to forgive and receive Onesimus as a brother in Christ, showing how the gospel transforms relationships.
themes: Forgiveness; reconciliation; brotherhood in Christ; love working through appeals rather than commands; Paul taking on another's debt

places: Colossae, Rome
keyverses: 1:6; 1:15-16; 1:18

## 59 James

## 62 1 John

## 65 Jude

## 66 Revelation

date: About AD 95, near the end of the emperor Domitian's reign

audience: Seven churches in the Roman province of Asia, and the church in every age
type: Prophecy and apocalyptic, in the form of a letter
background: Under Domitian, emperor worship was increasingly demanded, and Christians who refused to worship Caesar faced discrimination, exclusion from trade guilds, exile and death. John was exiled to Patmos, a small island off the coast of Asia Minor, "on account of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus". On the Lord's day he received visions of the risen Christ, who gave him messages for seven churches: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. Then John was shown the throne room of heaven and a series of visions of judgements (seals, trumpets and bowls), the conflict between God and the forces of evil, the fall of "Babylon", Christ's return, the final judgement, and the new heaven and new earth. Revelation uses symbolic numbers and images drawn heavily from the Old Testament, especially Daniel, Ezekiel, Zechariah and Isaiah.
purpose: To reveal Jesus Christ as the risen Lord who rules history, to encourage his people to stay faithful and overcome in persecution, and to assure them of his final victory and the new creation.
themes: The sovereignty of God; Jesus the Lamb who was slain and the conquering King; worship; faithful witness and overcoming; judgement; the defeat of Satan; the return of Christ; the new heaven and new earth; God dwelling with his people

- Vision of the risen Christ | 1:1-20

- The throne, the scroll and the Lamb | 4:1-5:14
- The seven seals | 6:1-8:5
- The seven trumpets | 8:6-11:19
- The woman, the dragon and the beasts | 12:1-14:20
- The seven bowls | 15:1-16:21
- The fall of Babylon | 17:1-19:10
- Christ's return, the thousand years and the judgement | 19:11-20:15
- The new heaven and new earth | 21:1-22:21
people: John, Jesus (the Lamb), the twenty-four elders, the four living creatures, the two witnesses, the woman, the dragon (Satan), the beast, the false prophet, Babylon, the bride

connections: Revelation brings the whole Bible story to its end, echoing Genesis: the tree of life (Gen 2:9; Rev 22:2), no more curse (Gen 3:17; Rev 22:3). Its images come from Daniel 7, Ezekiel 1 and 40-48, Zechariah 4 and Isaiah 65-66. Compare Jesus' teaching on the end in Matthew 24.
